Part 1: Treating Psychological Disorders

Biomedical and Psychological Treatments

Two fundamentally different forms of therapy are used to treat psychological disorders: pyschotherapy and biomedical therapies. They are often used in combination.

  • Psychotherapy
    treatment of emotional, behavioral, and interpersonal problems through psychological techniques that promote understanding of problems and modify troubling feelings, behaviors, or relationships
    • there are several influential approaches that share the perspective that psychological factors play a significant role. However, they have different assumptions about the underlying causes of psychological problems
    • consequently, they use different strategies to produce beneficial changes in thoughs, feelings, and behaviors
  • Mental Health Providers
    • clinical psychologists, counseling psychologists, psychiatrists, psychoanalysts, licensed professional counselors, psychiatric social workers, marriage and family therapists, psychiatric nurses
  • Biomedical Therapies
    use of medications, electroconvulsive therapy, or other medical treatments to treat the symptoms associated with psychological disorders
    • assumed the symptoms of many psychological disorders involve biological factors, such as abnormal brain chemistry
  • Mental Health Providers
    licensed physicians and a limited number of specially trained psychologists can prescribe psychotropic medications (drugs used to treat psychological disorders)

Different Perspectives on the Treatment of Psychological Disorders

there are many perspectives on the treatment of psychological disorders. Different approaches to treatment are grounded in contrasting assumptions about the underlying cause of psychological disorders. This section will examine:

  • Psychoanalytic Therapy
  • Humanistic Therapy
  • Behavior Therapy
  • Cognitive Therapy

Psychoanalytic Therapy

Psychoanalysis: a type of psychotherapy originated by Sigmund Freud in which free association and transference are used to explore repressed or unconscious impulses, anxieties, and internal conflicts

What is the underlying cause of psychological problems?

  • Assumptions:
    Early childhood experiences provide the foundation for personality development. When early experiences result in unresolved conflicts, emotionally charged memories are repressed. Psychological problems are caused by unconscious (repressed) conflicts
    • repressed conflicts continue to influence thoughts and behaviors, including the dynamics of relationships with others

How does the therapist address the underlying cause?

  • Goal:
    make the unconscious conscious
    • insight enables the resolution of previously repressed conflicts

Techniques to Reveal and Resolve the Unconscious Conflicts

  • Free association: the patient spontaneously reports all thoughts, mental images, and feelings
  • Resistance: the unconscious attempts to block the revelation of reperssed memories and conflicts
  • Interpretation: a carefully timed explanation of the patient's dreams, feelings, or behaviors to help explore unconscious conflicts or motivations
  • Transference: emotions and desires associated with a significant person (such as a parents) are unconsciously transferred onto the psychologist

All these psychoanalytic techniques are designed to help the patient achieve insight into how past conflicts influence current behavior and relationships. The patient can then replace maladaptive emotional and behavioral patterns with adaptive ones.

Short-Term Dynamic Therapies

While traditional psychoanalysis may require 3 or more sessions per week over the span of years, short-term dynamic therapies promote beneficial changes in a few weeks or months

  • based on psychoanalytic theory
  • the patients' problems are quickly assessed at the beginning of therapy
  • therapist and patient agree on specific, concrete, and attainable goals
  • in actual sessions, most therapists are more directive than traditional psychoanalysts

Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

A brief psychodynamic psychotherapy that focuses on current relationships

  • it is based on the assumption that psychological symptoms are caused and maintained by interpersonal problems
  • it is used to treat eating disorders, substance-use disorders, major depressive disorder
  • it's also helpful for interpersonal problems

Humanistic Therapy

Client-Centered Therapy (Person-Centered Therapy)

A type of psychotherapy developed by Carl Rogers in which the therapist is nondirective and reflective, and the client directs the focus of each therapy session

  • Carl Rogers deliberately used the term "client" instead of "patient" because the word patient implied that the person in therapy was sick and needed to be cured by an authority figure

Assumptions:

The humanistic perspective on psychology emphasizes human potential, self-awareness, and freedom of choice

  • important factors in personality are the individual's conscious and subjective perception of self
  • people are seen as basically good and motivated toward self-actualization, the realization of the unqiue potentials and talents
  • psychological problems emerge when a person's actions clash with an inflexible, distorted, self-concept
    • the unhealthy, distorted self-concept and worldview came from conditional positive regard

How does the therapist address the underlying cause? Goal:

help the person develop a healthy self-concept and worldview by facilitating greater self-awareness, self-acceptance, and self-determination. Techniques to do so:

  • the therapist's role is to create conditions that allow the client to direct the focus of the therapy and pursue self-actualization; to create these conditions, the therapist must demonstrate:
    • Genuineness: therapist honestly and openly shares thoughts and feelings with client
    • Unconditional positive regard: therapist must value, accept, and care for client
    • Empathetic understanding: therapist must communicate and listen actively for personal meaning

Motivational Interviewing (MI)

A more recent therapeutic approach based on the Humanistic Perspective; helps clients overcome mixed feelings or reluctance about committing to change

  • usually lasts only one or two sessions and applied to a range of issues, such as addictions and techniques to improve health
  • client's self-motivated statements ("change-talk") are encouraged and strengthened
  • therapist uses empathetic undersranding and reflective listening to help clients exploration of personal values and motivations for change

Behavior Therapy

Psychodynamic and humanistic therapies are insight oriented. These perspectives assume greater self-understanding and awareness will lead to growth and change. In contrast, behavior therapists do not consider insight to be necessary or sufficient for promoting change

Behavior Therapy: a type of psychotherapy that focuses on directly changing maladaptive behavior patterns by using basic learning principles and techniques or behavior modification

What is the underlying cause of psychological problems? Assumptions:

Psychological problems are caused by the aquisition of maladaptive behaviors. Maladaptive behaviors are learned.

How does the therapist address the underlying cause? Goal:

Maladaptive behaviors can be reduced and replaced with adaptive behaviors through the application of learning principles (classical conditioning, operant conditioning, observational learning).

Techniques Based on Classical Conditioning

  • Counterconditioning: behavior therapy technique that involves learning a new response that in incompatible with a previously learned response
    • pioneered by Mary Cover Jones, who was a student of John Watson
    • she successfully treated 3-year-old Peter's fear of rabbits
      • the rabbit was repeatedly presented while Peter had a favorite snack
  • Exposure Therapy: behavioral therapy for phobias, panic disorder, PTSD, or related anxiety disorders in which the person is repeatedly exposed to the disturbing object or situation under controlled conditions
    • one widely used exposure therapy is systematic desensitization: type of behavior therapy that involves learning a new conditioned response (relaxation) that is incompatible with or inhibits the former conditioned response (fear and anxiety)
      • developed in the 1950s by Joseph Wolpe
      • based on the principle of counterconditioning
      • relaxation is incompatible with fear. One cannot be deeply relaxed and highly anxious at the same time.
      • systematic desensitization 3 steps:
        1. Learn progressive relaxation: develop skills to induce a deeply relaxed state
        2. contruct an anxiety hierarchy (AKA exposure hierarchy) of anxiety: provoking images arranged from least to most feared
        3. proceed up the hierarchy and vividly imagine imagine each scene while maintaining a state of relaxation
      • in practice, systematic desensitization is often combined with other techniques, such as observational learning
      • once desensitization is achieved in visualizations, therapy may progress to in vivo systematic desensitization ("real-life" practice)
        • sometimes Virtual Reality (VR) therapy is used
          • VR therapy consists of computer-generated scenes that you view wearing goggles and a special motion sensitive headset
          • VR therapy was first used for specific phobias (e.g., flying, heights, spides, etc.)
          • it has becomes an accepted treatment and is now being used for a variety of disorders

Techniques Based on Operant Conditioning

Behavior therapists have developed several treatments derived from B.F. Skinner's operant conditioning model of learning

  • Shaping involves reinforcing successive approximations of a desired behavior
  • Positive and negative reinforcement are used to increase the incidence of desired behaviors
  • Extinction, or the absence of reinforcement, is used to reduce the occurence of undersired behaviors

The first step in a treatment program is to identify specific problem behaviors and determine their baseline rate

The therapist then targets each problem behavior and objectively measures the progress toward specific behavioral goals

Token Economy: a form of behavior therapy in which the therapeutic environment is structured to reward desired behaviors with tokens or points that may eventually be exchanged for tangible rewards

  • effective in institutional settings (classrooms, inpatient psychiatric units, group homes, etc.) where there is ongoing observation and supervision

Cognitive Therapies

a group of psychotherapies based on the assumption that psychological problems are due to illogical patterns of thinking; techniques focus on recognizing and altering unhealthy thinking patterns

  • ex: Rational-Emotive Behavioral Therapy, developed by Albert Ellis and Cognitive Therapty (CT), developed by Aaron Beck

What is the underlying cause of psychological problems? Assumptions:

Psychological problems are caused by illogical and maladaptive thinking patters

How does the therapist address the underlying cause? Goal:

The therapist helps clients to learn to recognize and change unhealthy thinking patterns

Techniques:

Rational-Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) developed by Albert Ellis, and Cognitive Therapy (CT) devenloped by Aaron Beck are two examples

Rational-Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)

Developed by Albert Ellis, focuses on identifying and changing the client's core irrational beliefs

"People are not disturbed by things, but rather by their view of things." (Albert Ellis)

  • rational beliefs about events produce appropriate and proportional emotional responses and adaptive behaviors
  • irrational beliefs produce extreme emotional responses and maladaptive behaviors
  • uses the ABC model:
    • A = activting event
    • B = belief about the event
    • C = consequence (emotional consequence)

REBT Steps:

  1. Identify the core irrational beliefs that underlie personal distress
  2. The therapist vigorously disputes the irrational beliefs. REBT therapists tend to rely on logical persuasion and reason
  3. the long-term therapeutic goal is to teach clients to recognize and dispute their own irrational beliefs in a wide range of situations

REBT has been shown to be generally effective in treatment of major depressive disorder, and certain anxiety disorders. It is also useful in helping people overcome self-defeating behavior

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

Therapy that integrates cognitive and behavioral techniques and is based on the assumption that thought, moods, and behaviors are interrelated.

  • takes a pragmatic (practical) approach and combines the techniques that are most appropriate for an individuals specific problems
  • a vriety of applications across agre groups
    • effective for major depressive disorder, eating disorders, substance use disorders, and anxiety disorders
    • can also help decrease the incidence of delusions and hallucinations with schizophrenia and psychotic symptoms
  • an emerging approach in CBT involves the use of mindfulness techniques: Mindfulness-based therapies or minfulness and acceptance therapies
    • involve present-centered awareness without judgement
    • Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR): involves a structured program of mindfulness meditation, yoga and mindful body practices, and group discussion

Part 2: Evaluating Treatments

Evaluate the efficacy of treatments for psychological disorders.

Most people with psychological symptoms do not seek help form mental help professionals.

  • many people eventually weather their problems without professional intervention. Instead, they may seek help from family and friends.

Researchers study outcomes. People who enter psychotherapy are compared with a carefully selected, matched control group of people who do not receive psychotherapy.

  • researchers use meta-analysis to combine and interpert large numbers of studies
  • results show psychotherapy is significantly more effective than no treatment
  • on average, a perso who completes psychotherapy is better off than about 80% of those in the untreated control group
  • gains that people make as a result of psychotherapy tend to endure long after the therapy has ended
  • an "umbrella" meta-analysis by Leichsenring and colleagues (2022) combined many meta-analyses into one review and convincingly demonstrated the effectiveness of psychotherapy

Brain imaging technologies show that psychotherapy alone produces distinct physiological changes in the brain that are associated with a reduction in symptoms

  • PET scans may show changes equivalent to drug therapies
    • e.g., one study demonstrated these changes after 10 weeks of psychotherapy for OCD

Nevertheless, psychotherapy is not a miracle cure. No everyone benefits from psychotherapy to the same degree.

Is One Form of Psychotherapy Superior?

In come cases, one type of psychotherapy is more effective for a particular problem.

  • cognitive, cognitive-behavioral, and behavior therapies tend to be more successful than insight-oriented therapes for panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, phobias, and PTSD

However, meta-analyses show little or no difference in the effectiveness of different psychotherapies.

  • The fact that little differences exist in outcomes among empirically supported psychotherapies does not mean that untested therapies are effective
  • Empirically supported treatments:
    • based on known psychological principles
    • have been tested in controlled scientific trials
    • demonstrated effectiveness
  • using the scientific metod to empirically test therapies takes time, but it is essential to establish safety and efficacy.
  • Beware of untested psychotherapies!
    • Rebirthing Therapist Sentenced to 16 years in prisonafter the death of a 10-year-old girl who begged for air and screamed for mercy after she was bound head-to-toe in a flannel sheet during a discredited psycotherapy procedure called "rebirthing".

Research shows common factors that correlate with positive outcomes:

  • the quality of the therapeutic relationship: mutual respect, trust, hope, common goals (the most important factors)
  • therapist characteristics: caring attitude, empathetic listening, genuine commitment to the client's well-being
  • cultural sensitivity: awareness of biases, has multicultural training

Increasingly, mental health professionals are moving toward eclecticism: the pragmatic and integrated use of techniques from different psychotherapies

  • eclectic psychotherapists carefully tailor the therapy approach to the problems and characteristics of the person seeking help
