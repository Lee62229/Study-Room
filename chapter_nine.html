Part 1: Factors in Development

The Role of Sensitive and Critical Periods in Development

Developmental Psychology: the study of how people change physically, cognitively, and socially throughout the lifespan.
Developmental psychologists investigate the influence of many factors on development at every stage of life:

  • biological
  • environmental
  • social
  • cultural
  • behavioral

Developmental psychologists often conceptualize the lifespan in terms of basic development.
Human Developmental Stages:

  • Prenatal: conception to birth
  • Infancy & Toddlerhood: birth to 2 years
  • Early Childhood: 2 to 6 years
  • Middle Childhood: 6 to 12 years
  • Adolescence: 12 to 18 years
  • Emerging Adulthood: 18 to 25 years
  • Young Adulthood: 25 to 40 years
  • Middle Adulthood: 40 to 65 years
  • Late Adulthood: 65 years to death

Some transitions in life may be abrupt, however, most are gradual
Critical periods and sensitive periods refer to periods during which a child is maximally sensitive to environmental influences (ex: prenatal development, language development, etc.)

The Interaction of Environmental and Biological Factors in Development

An important theme in development psychology is the interaction between heredity and environment, which is traditionally called the nature-nurture issue

Key Terms:

  • Zygote: the single cell formed at conception from the union of the egg and sperm cell
  • Genotype: the genetic makeup of an individual organism
  • Phenotype: the observable traits or characteristics of an organism
    • determined by the interaction of genetics and environmental factors

Different genotypes react differently to environmental factors, which sculpts gene expression

  • genes don't "make" your behavior; they provide the recipe for the proteins that build the "machinery" (your brain and body) that produces that behavior
  • experience affects which genes are turned on and off
  • people have genetic predispositions to develop in a particular way

Epigenetics: the study of cellular mechanisms that control gene expression and of the ways that gene expression affects health and behavior

  • gene expression can be triggered by the activitiy of other genes, internal chemical changes, or external environmental factors

Review Questions:

  • What is a sensitive period in development?

  • Which of the following is an example of a sensitive period in human development?

  • How do environmental factors influence gene expression according to the study of epigenetics?
    • Environmental factors can activate or deactivate certain genes, influencing traits and behaviors.
  • Which term describes the observable traits and characteristics of an organism, shaped by both genetics and the environment?
    • Phenotype
  • What does the term "nature–nurture issue" refer to in developmental psychology?
    • The examination of the relative contributions and interactions among genetic factors and environmental factors in development.

Part 2: Development Across the Lifespan: Early Development

The Major Physical, Cognitive, and Psychological Changes Across the Lifespan, including Influences on Prenatal Development

Prenatal Development

  1. Germinal (Zygotic) period: first two weeks after conception
    • rapid cell division
    • implantation on uterine wall
  2. Embryonic period: weeks three through eight after conception
    • the organs and major systems of the body form
    • greatest vulnerability to teratogens
  3. Fetal period: the ninth week until birth
    • body systems grow and mature

Teratogens: harmful agents or substances that can cause malformations or defects in an embryo or fetus. Examples:

  • radiation exposure
  • toxic chemicals and metals
  • viruses and bacteria
  • prescription and nonprescription drugs
  • addictive drugs
  • maternal smoking and secondhand smoking
  • Alcohol: excessive drinking may cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders
    • symptoms include: abnormal facial features, poor coordination, learning disabilities, bahavior problems, and intellectual disability
  • mother's psychological state, unhealthy behaviors

Infancy and Childhood

Newborn Reflexes and Senses

Initially, the newborn's behavior is mostly limited to reflexes that enhance survival. Over the first year of life, these reflexes are replaced by voluntary behaviors.

  • Rooting: turning the head and opening the mouth in the direction of a touch on the cheek
  • Sucking: sucking rhythmically in response to oral stimulation
  • Grasping: the baby will grip your finger so tightly that he can be lifted upright

The newborn's senses are keenly attuned to people, helping the infant quickly learn to differentiate between the mother and other humans

  • within just a few hours of birth, newborns display a preference fro their mother's voice and face over those of a stranger
  • vision is the least developed sense at birth. Optimal viewing distance for the newborn is approximately 6 to 12 inches, which is the perfect distance for a nursing baby to easily focus on his mother's face and make eye contact

Infant/Child Physical Development

  • during infancy, the brain will grow to ~75% of its adult weight
  • physical and motor skill development follows a top-to-bottom sequence
  • for most of their body, infacts develop motor control from the center of the body, outward
  • the basic sequence of motor skill development is universal, but the average ages can be a little deceptive
    • rolling over: 2-5 months
    • grasping rattle: 2.5-4.5 months
    • sitting without support: 5-8 months
    • standing while holding on: 5-10 months
    • standing alone well: 10-14 months
    • walking well: 11-14.5 months
    • building tower of cubes: 12-20 months
    • walking up steps: 14-22 months

Infant/Child Personality and Psychological Development

Temperament: inborn predispositions to consistently behave and react in a certain way.
Factors that influence temperament:

  • individual differences in temperament have a genetic and biological basis
  • environmental experiences can modify a child's basic temperament
  • infant temperament can also be affected by cultural beliefs

In the 1950's, Thomas and Chess rated young infants on a variety of characteristics, such as activitiy level, mood, regularity in sleeping and eating, and attention span.
Babies were classified as:

  • Easy: adaptable, positive mood, regular habits
  • Slow-to-Warm-Up: low activity, somewhat slow to adapt, generally withdraw from new situations
  • Difficult: intense emotions, irritable, cry frequently
  • Average: unable to classify (1/3 of all children)

After studying the same individuals from infancy through childhood, Thomas and Chess (1986) found the temperament patterns to be remarkably stable over time.
Virtually all temperament researchers agree that temperament has a genetic and biological basis, but that environmental experiences can modify a child's basic temperament.

Attachment: the emotional bond that forms between the infant and caregivers, especially parents, during the first year of life

  • infants can form multiple attachments

after WWII, psychiatrist Rene Spitz documented the effects of institutionalization on chicldren whose physical needs were met, but failed to thrive because they lacked interation with a warm and nurturing caregiver.

Harry Harlow showed that it wasn't just human chidlren who suffered from the lack of care. Infant rhesus monkeys who were raised in isolation from other monkeys showed sever pathology

  • baby monkeys would cline to the cloth-covered figure, even though it did not provide food, rather than the wire mother
  • the conclusion: all primates, including humans, seek out "contact comfort"

According to the attachment theory developed by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, an infant's ability to thrive physically and psychologically depends in part on the quality of attachment
Infants can form secure or insecure attachments:

  • Secure attachment: occurs when parents/caregivers are consistently warm, responsive, and sensitive to their infant's needs
    • cross-cultural studies have confirmed that sensitivity to the infant's needs is associated with secure attachment in diverse cultures
  • Insecure attachment: may develop when an infant's parents/caregivers are neglectful, inconsistent, or insensitive to the infant's moods or behaviors
    • reflects an ambivalent or detached emotional relationship

The Strange Situation was developed by Ainsworth to measure attachment. It is typicaly used with infants between 1 and 2 years old

  1. the baby and mother are placed in an unfamiliar room for a few minutes with a variety of toys
  2. a stranger enters the room
  3. the mother stays with the child for a few moments, and then departs, leaving the child with the stranger
  4. after a few minutes, the mother returns, spends a few minutes in the room, leaves, and returns again

The baby is observed through a one-way window and attachment is assessed based on the baby's behaviors towards the mother

  • Securely attached infants:
    • used the mother as a secure base for exploration when she is present
    • shows distress when she leaves the room
    • greets her warmly when she returns
  • Insecurely attached infants:
    • are less likely to explore the new environment when the mother is present
    • may become extremely distressed when she leaves OR appear indifferent
    • may be extremely difficult to console OR avoid her when she returns

The quality of attachment during infancy is associated with a variety of long-term effects.
For example, secure attachment in infancy predicts:

  • Pre-school: greter empathy, social competence, and more prosocial behavior
  • Adolescence: fewer problems, more successful relationships, more academic success

Infant/Child Cognitive Development

Cognitive development changes in the way we process information

Jean Piaget, a Swiss psychologist believed:

  • children are active learners
  • children progress through 4 distinct cognitive stages
  • as a child advances to a new stage, thinking is qualitatively different
  • a new understanding of the world is developed in each progressive stage
  • based on understandings acquired in the previous stage, children:
    • assimilate new information and experiences
    • change their way of thinking to accommodate new knowledge

Piaget believed the four stages were biologically programmed and universal. However, heredity and environment could influence the rate at which children progress through the stages

  1. Sensorimotor Stage
    • Ages: birth to 2 years
    • The infant explores the environment primarily through sensing and manipulating objects
    • Achievements: infants gradually acquire object permanence (the understanding that an object continues to exist even if it can't be seen) as they gain experience with objects, as their memory abilities improve, and as the develop mental representations of the world, which Piaget called schemas
  2. Preoperational Stage
    • Ages: 2 to 7 years
    • stage characterized by incresing use of symbols and pre-logical though processes
    • thinking is characterized by:
      • Egocentrism: inability to take the perspective of another
      • Irreversibility: inability to reverse a sequence
      • Centration: tendency to focus on one aspect and ignore other important aspects
    • Achievements: Symbolic though (the ability to use words, images, and symbols to represent the world), evident in language skills and symbolic/fantasy play
  3. Concrete Operational Stage
    • Ages: 7 to 11 years
    • child now has the ability to think logically about concrete objects and situations
    • Achievements" reversibility, logic for concrete situations, classification, conservation
      • conservation: the understanding that 2 equals quantities remail equal even though the appearance changes (as long as nothing is added or subtracted)

Part 3: Development Across a Lifespan: Adolescence & Beyond

The Major Physical, Cognitive, and Psychosocial Changes across the Lifespan, continued.

Adolescence

  • transitional stage between alte childhood and early adulthood
  • sexual maturity is reached

Adolescent Physical and Sexual Development

Puberty: stage of adolescence in which individual reches sexual maturity, becoming physiologically capable of sexual reproduction

  • Primary sex characteristics: sexual organs that are directly involved in reproduction
    • ex: the uterus and the testes enlarge in puberty
  • Secondary sex characteristics: sexual characteristics that develop during puberty and are not directly involved in reproduction, but differentiate between the sexes
    • ex: change in height, weight, and body shape; the appearance of body hair; voice changes
  • Adolescent growth spurt: period of accelerated growth during puberty, involving rapid increases in height and weight
    • about 2 years earlier in females
  • Menarche: a female's first menstrual period, which occurs during puberty

Adolescent Cognitive Development

Piagets Theory: Stage 4: Formal Operational Stage

  • Ages: adolescence through adulthood
  • the stage characterized by the ability to think logically about abstract principles and hypothetical situations
  • achievements: ability to take a broader and more theoretical approach to questions of ethics, politics, and moral issues

Adolescent Psychosocial Development

Adolescence is a time of changes in social interactions. Research shows:

  • Parent-child relationships are overall positive:
    • conflict increases during this time; this is seen as healthy as autonomy increases
    • relationships that were good in childhood continue to be good
  • Friends and peers become more of an influence:
    • influence peaks during early adolescence
    • tend to reinforce the traits and goals that parents fostered during childhood
  • Romantic and sexual relationship become increasingly important
    • social and cultural factors influence when, why, and how adolescents engage in romantic and sexual behaviors

Identity: a person's sense of self, including memories, experiences, and values and beliefs that guide behavior.
According to Erik Erikson, the key pyschosocial conflict facing adolescents is "Identity versus Role Confusion"

  • the adolescent's path to successful identity achievement begins with role confusion (little sense of committment)
  • this is followed by a moratorium period (experimenting with different roles, values, and beliefs)

Gradually, the adolescent arrives at an integrated identity (committments to roles, values, and beliefs yielding a coherent sense of self). THis is a successful resolution of the Psychosocial conflict

According to Erkison, adolescents who don't form an integrated identity maintain some degree of role confusion

Adult Development (Emerging Adulthood through Late Adulthood)

According to developmental psychologist Jeffrey Arnett, the years from the late teens through the 20s represent a distinct developmental stage: emergin adulthood:

  • Occurs in industrialized countries
  • characterized by exploration and flexibility in social roles, vocational choices, and relationships
  • identity not full resolved until mid- or late 20s
  • marriage is often postponed until the late 20s or even 30s, after education is complete and careers are established

Adult Physical Changes

  • physical strength typically peaks in early adulthood: the 20s and 30s
  • by middle adulthood, roughly from the 40s to the mid-60s, physical strength and endurance gradually decline, and mental reaction times also begin to slow
  • during late adulthood, from the mid-60s on, physical stamina and reaction time tend to decline further and faster

Reproductive and Hormonal Changes:

  • Menopause in Women:
    • natural cessation of menstruation
    • signals the end of reproductive capacity
    • occurs any time from the late 30s to the early 50s
  • Andropause in Men:
    • do not experience an abrupt end to their reproductive capacity
    • do experience a gradual decline in testosterone levels
    • loss of lean muscle, increased body fat, weakened bones, reduced sexual function and motivation, and cognitive decline may occur

Adult Psychosocial Changes

According to Erikson's Psychosocial Stages of Development, the psychosocial conflict of young adulthood is "Intimacy vs Isolation". A successful resolution of this conflict is the development of lasting and meaningful relationships
The "traditional" path of achieving intimacy in adulthood was to find a mate, get married, and start a family. Social Development doesn't always follow a predictable pattern.

According to Erikson's Psychosocial Stages of Development, the psychosocial conflict of Middle Adulthood is "Generativity vs Stagnation" A successful resolution means that the individual expresses unselfish concern for the next generation through child rearing, caring for others, productive work, and/or community involvement.

Transition to parenthood

  • marital satisfaction and time together tend to decline after the birth of the first child and rise again after the children leave the home
  • becoming a parent at an older age and waiting longer after marriage to start a family help ease the adjustment to parenthood
  • dual-career families have become increasingly common
  • married women with children are more likely than single women or childless women to interrupt their careers, leave their jobs, or switch to part-time work because of child-rearing responsibilities
  • although many fathers are actively involved in child rearing, women in heterosexual couple still tend to have primary responsibility for childcare and other types of household labor
  • multiple roles seem to provide both men and women with a greater potential for increased seld-esteem, happiness, and competence/ The critical factor is not the number of roles that people take on, but the quality of their experiences on the job, in the marriage, and as a parent

According to Erkison's Psychosocial Stages of Development, the psychosocial conflict of late adulthood is "Ego Integrity vs Despair". A successful resolution of the conflict enables an individual to look back on life with a sense of self-acceptance and the feeling that one's life has been meaningful.

According to the activity theory of aging, life satisfaction in late adulthood is highest when people maintain their previous level of activity.

  • for many older adults, caregiving responsibilities (for grandchildren or other older adults) can persist well into late adulthood
  • contrary to stereotypes, most older adults ive healthy, active, and self-sufficient lives

Adult Cognitive Changes

Psychologist K. Warner Schaie and his colleagues have found taht general intellectual abilities remain relatively stable until approximately age 60, when a small, but steadily increasing percentage of older adults experience slight declines.

  • Decline in mental abilities is often because of lack of practice or experience
  • Some researchers suggests neurons become less efficient in older brains, resulting in slower processing

Research has found that those who are better educated and engaged in physical, mental,a dn social activityies throughout older adulthood showed the smallest declines in mental abilities
