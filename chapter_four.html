Part 1: Consciousness and Attention

Consciousness and its Various States

Consciousness: personal awareness of mental activities, internal sensations, and the external environment

  • understanding consciousness was the central focus of psychology at its inception. Wilhelm Wundt defined psychology as the study on consiousness
  • consciousness is critical to our sophisticated cognitive abilities, such as planning and problem solving. It allows us to integrate past experiences with out present behaviors and guide out future actions as well
  • the personal awareness of consciousness is often captivated by specific things

Attention: the capacity to selectively focus senses and awareness on particular stimuli in the environment or on internal thoughts and sensations

attention had several important limitatios:

  • capacity: we cannot focus on everything simultaneously. we generally select the info most relevant to our goals
  • selective: this is illustrated by the cocktail party effect
    • it's a noisy environment with many simultaneous conversations, we selectively focus on our own. howerver, if we hear our own name mentione in another coversation, we immediately shift focus to the other conversation and briefly nreglect our own
  • blindness: this is illustrated by inattentional blindness
    • we sometimes fail to notice something significant that's clearly in our field of vision

Multitasking: paying attention to two or more sources of stimuli at once

  • it involves division of attention. Shifting attention frequently among different tasks for concurrent activities causes more error and is less efficient than completing activities sequentially
  • tasks that are very fdifferent, especially those that call on a different sense, are less likely to interfere with each other. However, this is not the casde when one of the tasks requires a great deal of concentration

we often associate alert, focused attention with the term consciousness. However, consciousness presents iteself in a variety of states

  • ex: consciousness varies according to circadian rhythms, specifically the sleep-wake cycle
  • various practices and substances can also produce profound alteration in consciousness
    • ex: hypnosis, meditation, and psychoactive drugs can all create their own discernable variation in consciousness

Part 2: Sleep

Characteristic of Sleep and Theories that Explain Why we Sleep and Dream

Circadian Rhythms

consciousness, like many other bodily functions, varies in a systematic way over the course of every 24-hour period

Circadian Rhythm: a roughly 24-hour long cycle of fluctuation in biological and psychological processes

a tiny cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus controls the timing of circadian rhythms

Environmental cues: help keep circadian rhythms synchronized

  • sunlight: the most important cue, and other bright light is detenced by visual receptors and communicated to the hypothalamus, which communicates with the pineal gland
  • Melatonin: a hormon that promotes sleepiness is prduced by the pineal gland
    • bright light suppresses hte production of melatonin
    • sunset (decreasing light) triggers the production of melatonin

Exposure to environmental time signals is necessary to stay entrained to a 24-hour day. When deprived of all environmental time cues:

  • people rift toward the natural rhythm of the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which is about 24.2 hours, or slightly longer than a day
  • circadian rhythms lose their normal synchronization with one another
  • Jet lag: occurs when your certaian circadian rhythms are drastically out of synchronization with daylight and darkness cues
    • symptoms include physical and mental fatigue, depression or irritability, and disrupted sleep. In addition, thinking, concentration, and memory become fuzzy

Sleep Research

modern sleep research began with Han's berger's invention of the electroencephalograph in the 1920s

  • it gave sleep researchers an important tool for measuring hte rhythmic electrical activity of the brain (brain waves)
  • it produces a graphic record called an EEG, or elecroencephalogram
  • this research showed that the brain remains activing during sleep, but the pattern of activity differs from the waking state

today, sleep researchers distinguish betwen two basic types of sleep:

  • REM (rapid eye movement) sleep: sleep during which rapid eye movements (REM) and dreaminf usually occur and voluntary muscle ctivity is supressed
  • NREM (non-rapid-eye-movement) sleep: quiet sleep, typically dreamless, in which rapid eye movemetns are absent; divided into 3 stages

sleep invovles a series of stages characterized by changes in brain waves:

  • the stages repear in cycles
  • each cycle last approximately 90 mins
  • Beta brain waves: small, fast brain waves associated with alert wakefulness
  • Alpha brain waves: slightly larger and slower waves associated with relaxed wakefulness and drowsiness
  • Hypnagogic hallucinations: vivid sensory phenomena that can occur during the onset of sleep (ex: the sensation of falling)

Stage 1 NREM sleep: begins when alpha brain waves are replaced by even slower theta brain waves. this stage only lasts a few minutes

  • disengagement from sensations of the surrounding world
  • conscious alertness can be quickly regained if needed

Stage 2 NREM sleep: represents the onset of true sleep. It is defined by sleep spindles and K complexes. Theta waves predominate, but delta waves begin to emerge

  • sleep spindles: bried bursts of brain activity that last a second or two
  • K complex: single but large high-voltage spike of brain activity

Stage 3 NREM sleep: sometimes referred to as slow-wave sleep (SWS), is physiologically similar to Stage 2 NREM slleep. Delta brain waves eventually come to represent 100% of brain activity

  • heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing rate drop to their lowest levels
  • it may take 15 minutes or longer to fully wake up

after stage 3, the sequence briefly reverses as the sleeper moves from stage 3 to stage 2 in a matter of mints and enters REM sleep

during REM sleep: there is increased psysiological arousal and brain actiivty

  • heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing rate fluctuate
  • brain waves are smaller and faster
  • voluntary muscle activity is suppressed. if the sleeper wakes up from REM before voluntary muscle functioning returns, sleep paralysis may be experienced. this sensation of being unable to move may last for up to 10 minutes

Theories that Explain Why we Sleep

sleep researcher aren't really sure why we sleep

  • sleep initiates a process for clearing metabolic waste products from the brain
  • sleep isn thought to maintain immune system function, enhance learning and consolidate memory, improve brain funciton, and help regulate moods and emotions

sleep may play a special role in preserving personal and emotional memories

  • new memories from the day are reactivated during th sleep cycles throughout the night. The repeated process of activating these neural connections helps consolidate (solidify) the newly encoded memories
  • these processes also help integrate the new memories into existinf networks of info

many species have vastly different sleep patterns prointing to the role of evolutionary adaptation

  • different sleep patterns evolved as a way of conserving energy and preventing a aprticular species from interacting with the environment when doing so is most hazardous

a biological need for sleep is clearly demonstrated by sleep deprivation studies

  • after as little as one night's of sleep deprivation, research participants develop microsleeps, episodes of sleep lasting only a few seconds during wakefulness
  • people who go without sleep for a day or more experience disruptions in mood, metnal abilities, reaction time, perceptual skills, adn complex motor skills

Dream Research

researches also study mental activity during sleep:

Sleep thinking: repetitive, bland, uncreative rumination about real -life events, occurs far more frequently than dreams, usually during NREM slow wave sleep

dream: an unfolding sequence of perceptions, thoughts, and emotions during sleep that are experienced as a series of real-life events, most occur during REM sleep

  • when awakened during REM sleep, epole report a dream about 90% of the time
  • people usually have four or five dreaming episodes each night

Willian Dumhoff's research on dream themes and imagery dispelled some myths and reveals some common dream themes

  • so-called common dreams are actually quite rare
  • most dreams are about everyday situations
  • negative emotions are more common than positive. Fear is most commonly reported dream emotion
  • aggression is more common than friendliness; dreamers are more likey to be victims of aggression than aggressors

nightmare: vivid and distrubing dream that often awakens the sleeper

  • fear, anxiety, adn terror are the most comomnly experienced emotions

Theories tht Explain Why we Dream

Siigmund Freud: founded of psycholoanalysis, believed that dreams are manifestations of repressed urges and wishes

  • dreams areve as a "safety valve" for unconsious and unacceptable urges. they fulfill wishes and usrges in a way that perserves psychological and physical safety
  • freud suggested that dreams have two components
    • Manifest content: the actual dreams themselves
    • latent content: the diguised psychological meaning of the dreams
  • although dreams may provide some info about psychological conflicts, research does not support Freud's ideas about dreams

Activatin-Synthesis model: prposed by J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley, dreajs are the subjective awareness of internally generated signals

  • Activation: automatic activation of brainstem circuits during REM sleep arouse higher brain regions including hte limbic system as well as visual, motor, and auditory pathways. a variety of internal signals are generated
    • : more sophisticated brain areas combine these internally generated sensory signals into a dream story
  • this theory doe sot state that dreams are meaningless. Meaning is found by analyszing the way the dreamer makes sense of the chaotic dream images

Neurocognitive Theory of Dreaming: proposed by William Domhoff, emphasizes the continuity between waking and dreaming cognition

  • Domhoff disagreed with the Activation-Synthesis model. He emphasized that dreams are not a "cognitive mishmash". Furthermore, he noted that drams are not a result of the brain activiation of REM
  • dreams can occur during NREM or at sleep onset
  • dreamline episodes can occur under other circumstances as well
  • dreams refelct each person's unique interests, concerns, and personality

Part 3: Hypnosis and Meditation

Hypnosis and its Effects

HypnosisA cooperative social interaction in which the hypnotized person responds to the hypnotist’s suggestions with changes in perception, memory, and behavior.

it's characterized by highly focused attention, increased responsiveness to suggesstions, vivid images and fantasies, a willingness to accept distortion of logic or reality, and a voluntary acceptance of the hynotists instruction

  • Responsiveness to hypnosis: the best candidates are people who feel postive towards and open to hypnosis
    • about 15% of adults are highly susceptible to hynosis
    • about 10% are more difficult or impossible
  • effects of hypnosis: laborator studies show hypnosis can cause sensory/perceptual changes
    • temporary blindness / deafness
    • numbinf of specific parts of the body (it can effectively control pain)
    • hallucinations
    • the effects can persist after th session ends through the use of post-hypnotic suggestions
      • suggestions made during hypnosis that the person will carry out a specific instruction after the hypnotic session. most of these suggestion last a few days or hours

psychologists disagree about whether hynosis is an altered (special) state of consciousness
