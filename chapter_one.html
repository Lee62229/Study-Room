Introduction and Research Methods

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes.

Four basic goals of psychology are to decribe, explain, and control or influence behavior and mental processes

Psychologists seek to go beyond intuitive or "common sense" understandings of human experience. For example, with stress:

  • Describe: trying to objectively describe the experience of stress, a psychologist studies the sequence of emotional responses that occur during stressful experiences
  • Predict: Investigating responses to different kinds of challenging events, a psychologist hopes to predict the kinds of events that are most likely to evoke a stress response
  • Explain: Seeking to explain why some people are more vulnerable to the effects of stress than others, a pychologist studies the different ways in which people respond to natural disasters
  • Control, change, or influence: after studying the effectiveness of different coping strategies, a psychologist helps people use those coping strategies to better control their reactions to stressful events.

The history of psychology is a field struggling to define itself as a seperate and unqiue scientific discipline. Early psychologists debated such fundamental issues as:

  • What is the proper subject matter of psychology?
  • What methods should be used to investigate psychological issues?
  • Should psychological findings be used to change or enhance human behavior?

These debates helped set the tone of the new science, define its scope, and set its limits.

Part 1: Psychology and its History

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. These are its primary objectives:

  • describe,
  • predict,
  • explain, and
  • control, change, or influence behavior and mental processes

Some of psychology's most enduring themes have their roots in philosophy.

  • early philosophers tried to understand the mind-body connection.
  • French philosopher, René Descartes proposed that the mind and body are seperate entities that interact to produce sensations, emotions, and other conscious experiences.
  • Psychologists continue to study the conenction between physiological processes and conscious experiences.
    • Is consciousness nothing more than the product of a complex mixture of chemical and electrical signals among the cells of your brain?

Philosophers also laid the groundwork for another issue that would become central to psychology, the nature-nurture issue.

  • For centuries, philosophersdebated which was more improtant: the inborn nature of the individual or the environmental influences that nurture the individual.
  • Today, pschologists recognize that nature and nurture cannot be completely seperated and compared. There is a greater focus on the interaction between genetics and environmental factors.

Wilhelm Wundt (1832-1920) was a German physiologist who used expiremental methods to study and measure basic psychological processes, such as reaction time. Ths was an attempt to measure basic aspects of human awareness or consciousness.

  • He opened the first psychology researcg laboratory at the University of Leipzig in 1879.
  • Often cited as the beginning of psychology as an expiremental science.
  • Wundt defined psychology as the study of consciousness.

Early psychologists tried to understand the nature of consciousness

  • Structuralisim: complex conscious experiences could be broken down intro elemental structures of sensations and feelings
    • The first major school of though in psychology
  • Functionalism: studied how behavior functions to allow people and animals to adapt to their environmetns. This perspective was influenced by Darwin.
  • Psychoanalysis: personality theory and form of psychotherapy
    • Sigmund Freud (1866-1939)
    • The consciousmind is just the tip of the iceberg!!!
    • Human behavior is motivated by unconscious conflicts that are generally sexual or aggresive in nature.
    • Early childhood experiences are critical in the formation of the adult personality
    • Powerful influence on later theories of psychology
  • Behaviorism: studied the fundamental principles of learning—how behavior is acquired and modified in response to environmental influences
    • grew out of the work of physiologist, Ivan Pavlov (1849-1936) who studied conditioning on dogs.
    • Later, American psychologist, John Watson (1878-1958) founded bahaviorism as a school of thought. This perspecticve was promoted by Watson and B.F. Skinner (1904-1990)
      • They believed psychology should restrict itself to studying outwardly observable behaviors that could be measured and verified in experiments.

By the early 1950s, structuralism and functionalism has disappeared along with their emphasis on consciousness

  1. Psychoanalysis emphasized the unconscious
  2. Behaviorism rejected the study of mental proceses

Humanistic psychology emerged as a "third force" in American psychology. It brough an emphasis on conscious choice and self-direction

  • Carl Rogers (1902-1987) emphasized the person's conscious experiences, unique potential for psychological growth and self-direction, self-determination, and free will
  • Abraham Maslow (1908-1970) proposed a theory of motivation that emphasized the importance of psychological growth

Part 2: Contemporary Perspectives and Diversity

  • biological perspective: emphasizes the psysical bases of behavior and mental processes. Researchers study the nervous system, the endocrine system, immune system, and genetics.
    • Neuroscience is the study of the nervous system, especially the brain
    • The development of sophisticated brain-scanning techniques has contributed to this contemporary perspective
  • psychodynamic perspective emphasizes the importance of unconscious influences, early life experience, and interpersonal relationships in explaining the underlying dynamics of behavior
    • based on Sigmun Freud's psychoanalytic theory
    • not all of Freud's ideas are embraced
  • behavioral perspective studies how behavior is acquired and modified through experience and environment
    • mental health professionals may emphasize the behavioral perspective in explaining and treating psychological disorders
  • humanistic perspective emphasizes the motivation for people to grow psychologically and the importance of choice and self-direction
    • influenced by the theories of Maslow and Rogers
    • focuses on personal growth, interpersonal relatioships, and self-concept
    • the humanistic perspective is often emphasized among psychologists working in the mental health field
  • Positive psychology focuses on the conditions and processes that contribute to the optimal functioning of people, groups and institutions
    • significantly influenced by the Humanistic perspective
    • topics include personal happiness, optimism, creativity, resilience, character strengths, and wisdom
    • this focus on well-being contrasts with psychology's traditional emphasis on pathology
  • cognitive perspective focuses on how people process and remember information, develop language, solve problems, and think
    • the development of computers in the 1950s contributed to "the cognitive revoltion" in psychology. This renewed interest in studying mental processes at a time when behaviorism had dominated psychology
  • evolutionary perspective emphasizes how the principles of evolution apply to psychological processes
    • ex: it examines how natural selection applies to psychological traits
      • organisms that inherit characteristics that increase their chances of survival in their particular habitat are more likely to survive, reproduce, and pass on their characteristics to their offspring
  • cross-cultural perspective emphasizes how cultural factors influence patterns of behavior
    • emphasizes diversity of behavior across cultures
    • emerged in the 1980s after some well-established research findings failed to replicate in other cultures
      • ex: study of social loafing

Part 3: The Scientific Method and Research Ethics

Scientific Method: a set of assumptions, attitudes, and procedures that guides all scientists, including psychologists, in conducting research.

  • Assumptions: events are lawful (follow consistent patterns) and explainable (have causes that can be udnerstoof through systematic study)

It is a process for producing empirical evidence (verifiable evidence based on objective observation, measurement, and expirementation). That process goes as follows:

  1. Formulate a Testable Hypothesis:
    • Hypothesis: a tentative statement about the relationship between two or more variables; a testable prediction or question
      • a variable is a factor that can vary or change in ways that can be observed, measured, and verified
      • each variable must be operationally defined
        • Operational definition: a precise description of how the variables in a study will be measured, manipulated, or changed.
  2. Design the study and collect data
    • there are two basic types of designs providing different types of evidence:
      • Descriptive research: strategies for observing and describing behavior. They can answer questions about when, where, and how often certain behaviors occur. Some descriptive data can determine if variables are related and some can be used to make predictions
      • Expiremental research: strategies for inferring cause and effect relationships among variables
  3. Analyze the data and draw conclusions
    • psychologists use statistics to summarize and make inferences about their data. Various statistical tests can be used to determine if the results are statistically significant
      • Statistical Significance: results are not very likely to have occured by chance
        • statistical significance does not necessarily mean real-world significance
  4. Report the results
    • Report: publish in a peer-reviewed journal or present at scientific conference:
      • the rationale for testing the hypothesis
      • who participated in the study
      • how participants were selected
      • how variables were operationally defined
      • what procedures or methods were used
      • how the data were analyzed
      • what the results seem to suggest
    • Benefits of reporting:
      • Peer review: other scientists review the manuscript
        • contructive criticism
        • safegaurds quality
        • Open science: the use of transparent research practices, including sharing the procedures of a study, the specific of how the statistics were calculated, and the research data. it is a way of crowdsourcing research
      • Replication: to repeat or duplicate a scientific study in order to increase confidence in the validity of the original findings
        • Replication crisis: many studies in psychology (as well as other fields) have failed to replicate
        • in one giant crowsourced study, 270 researchers from all over the world attempted to replicate 100 well-known psychology studies (Open Science Collaboration, 2015). Only 36 percent of these studies replicated.
        • exploring why a study did not replicate may lead to psychologists to a better understanding of the phenomenon they are studying
      • Development of theories: a tentative explanation that tries to integrate and account for the relationship of various findings and observations
        • a useful theory furthers the understanding of behavior / mental processes, allows testable predictions to be made, and stimulates new research
        • as new research findings are reported, theories can be expanded, modified, or even replaced
        • theories often reflect the self-correcting nature of the scientific enterprise

Some psychology studies are infamous for their failure to protect the dignity and welfare of their participants.

Therefore, the America Psychological Association (APA) developed ethics codes for research with humans and animals

At most institutions, research proposals must be evaluated and approved by an institutional review board.

APA ethics code for reseatch involving human participants: Five key provisions

  1. Informed consent and voluntary participation
    • psychologist must inform the participants of the purpose of the research, and any potential risks, discomfort, or unpleasant emotional experiences.
    • Participants are free to decline to participate or to withdraw from the research at any time without penalty
  2. Students as research participants
    • when research participation is a course requirement or an opportunity for extra credit, the student must be given the choice of an alternative activity to fulfill the course requirement or earn extra credit
  3. The use of deception psychologists can use deceptive techniques if:
    • it is not feasible to use alternatives that do not involve deception
    • the potential findings justify the use of deception because of their scientific, educational, or applied value

research with animals is far less common than with humans (typically 7-8% of research studies)

Comparative psychology focuses on the study of the behavior of nonhuman animals

  • some studies focus on the behaviors and cognitive processes of animals for the potential benefit to the animals themselves
    • ex: animal cognition examines learning, memory, thinking, and language in animals
  • some animal studies are conducted to identify basic principles that can be applied to humans
    • some research cannot feasibly be conducted on human participants

The APA publishes the Guidelines for Ethical Conduct in the Care and Use of Nonhuman Animals in Research.

  • psychologists also follow federal and state laws governing the use and care of research animals.

Part 4: Descriptive and Expiremental Methods

Descriptive research methods use scientific procedures to systematically observe and describe behavior, can produce conclusions about when, where, and how often certain behaviors occur. Some can determine if variables are related and some can be used to make predictions

  • Two basic research strategies are longitudal research and cross-sectional research.
    ex: a researcher has a hypothesis about how memory changes as people age from 20 to 50 years old.)
    • longitudal design tracks a variable or a set of variables in the same group of participants over time (even years)
      ex: recruit a group of 20-year-olds in 2026 and give them a memory test. Re-test that same group every 10 years until they reach age 50 in 2056. The researchers could observe how "Jane's" or "John's" memory specifically declines or improves over 30 years.
      • advantages and disadvantages:
        • Captures Growth: shows how specific individuals change or stay the same over time.
        • Temporal Sequencing: helps identify which behaviors or traits appear first in a sequence.
        • Expensive and Slow: requires massive resources and years of waiting for results
        • Participant Dropout: "attrition" can ruin sample if many people leave the study
    • Cross-sectional design studies a variable or set of variables among a group of participants at one point in time. Compares different groups (like different ages) at one time.
      ex: recruit four different groups of people right now: a group of 20-year-olds, 30-year-oldsm 40-year-olds, and 50-year-olds. Give them all the same memory test today. The researchers could compare the average score of the "20-year-old group" against the "50-year-old" group to see the difference.
      • advantages and disadvantages:
        • Fast & Cheap: data is collected all at once, providing immediate results
        • Low Commitment: no risk of losing participants over several years
        • The Cohort Effect: difference between groups may be due to their unique era (e.g., growing up with the internet vs. without) rather than age.
        • No Individual Flow: cannot show how an individual person actually develops
  • Naturalistic Observation: the systematic observation and recording of behaviors as they occur in their natural settings
    • Strengths and Limitations:
      • researchers directly observe and record behavior, rather than relying on subject descriptions
      • allows researchers to study human behaviors that cannot ethically be manipulated in an expirement
      • results are likely to generalize
      • can only be used in setting where behavior can openly be observed
      • researchers have no control; they cannot interact or interfere with their subects
    • prodecures to improve the credibility of the research findings:
      • behaviors must be carefully defined before they are observed and measured
      • often 2 researchers simultaneously conduct observations in order to compare data for accuracy
      • it is important for the researchers to avoid detection by their subjects because people change their behavior when they know they are being oberved
  • Case Study: an intensive, in-depth investigation of an individual, or some other social unit
    • strengths and limitations:
      • useful for studying rare, unusual, or extreme conditions that do not occur often enough to permit a large sample
      • can be helpful for understanding behavior and developing treatment for more common conditions
      • results may not generalize to the broader population
    • procedures to imprive the credibility of the research findings:
      • information is collected from a variety of sources to construct a comprehensive description
        • ex: interviews, testing, medical records, school records, etc.
  • Survey: a structured set of questions designed to investigate opinions, behaviors, or characteristics of a particular group.
    • usually in self-report form (questionnaire or interview)
    • strengths and limitations:
      • information can be gathered from large groups of people
      • response rates can be low
      • participants do not always answer honestly
    • procedures to improve the credibility of the research findings:
      • survey items must be carefully constructed
      • rephrase questuons to check for honesty and consistency
      • the sample should be representative, but this is difficult to achieve. Most research uses WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) societies.
        • Sample: a selected segment of the population used to represent the group that is being studied
        • representative sample: a selected segment that very closely parallels the larger population being studied on relevant characteristics
          • Random selection: every group member has an equal chance of being selected. This is an ideal, but difficult, strategy for achieving a representative sample
  • Correlation Study: examines how strongly two variables are related to, or associated with, each other
    this is not a strategy for collecting data, but for analyzing data.
    • Correlation Coefficient: numerical indication of magnitude and direction of the relationship between two variables
      • the number indicates the strength of the relationship
        look at the absolute value:
        • 0 = no relationship
        • as the absolute value approaches 1, the relationship is stronger
        • 1 = a perfect relationship
      • the sign indicates the direction of the relationship:
        • Positive Correlation (+) means the variables move in the same direction
        • Negative Correlation (-) means the variables move in the opposite direction
    • strengths and limitations:
      • can be used to rule out some factors and identify others for futher study
      • results can soemtimes allow meaningful predictions
      • cannot be used for cause-and-effect conclusions
      • directionality concerns
      • intervening (third) variables
    • procedures to improve the credibility of research findings:
      • accurate calculations will yield correlatuon coefficients between -1 and +1

Expiremental research methods is a method of investigation used to demonstrate cause-and-effect relationships by purposely manipulating one factor thought to produce change in another factor

  • Idependent Variable (predictor variable): is purposely manipulated
  • Dependent Variable (outcome variable): observed or measured for change
  • to the greated degree possible, all other factors are held constant
  • an expirement requires a minimu of 2 groups:
    • Experiment Group: group of participants exposed to the treatment condition
    • Control/Comparison Group: group of participants exposed to the control condition of the independent variable. They serve as a baseline for comparison against any chnages observed in the expiremental group
  • strengths and limitations:
    • a well-designed expirement can provide convicing evidence of a cause-and-effect relationship betwee the independent and dependent variables
      • an expirement is the only research design that cna produce this type of conclusion
    • expirements are often criticized for having little to do with actual behavior because of strict laboratory conditions.
    • results may not generalize
    • there are also ethical considerations increting some more lifelike situations
  • researchers may get more "real world" insights from "natural experiments": researchers carefully observe and measure the impact of a naturally occuring event or condition on their study participants
  • prodecures to improve the credibility of the research findings:
    • researcher nust manticipate sources of bias/error that can detract from the main effect (the effect od the IV on the DV). Such sources could be:
      • Practice effect: changes in performance on an assessment (e.g., post-test) due to previos experience with it (e.g., pre-test)
      • Confounding variables: extraneous variables that are not the focus of the expirement, but could affect the outcome if they're not controlled
      • Placebo effect: any change attributed to a person's beliefs and expectations, rather than the actual treatment
        • placebo: fake substance, treatment, or procedure with no known direct effects
      • Demand Characteristics: subtle cues or signals by the researcher that communicate the kind of response or behaviors that is expected from the participant
    • researcher use a variety of methods to minimize bias/error. These are called expiremental controls
      • the use of control groups and/or placebo control groups
        • Placebo control group: group of participants who receive a fake treatment or procedure that has no known direct effects
      • random assignment: all participants have an equal chance of being assigned to any of the expiremental groups or conditions
        • this helps ensure that any potential differences among the participants are spread out evenly across all experimental conditions
      • Double-blind technique: both the participants and the researches interacting with them are "blind", or unaware of the treatment or condition to which the participants have been assigned

Critical thinking can help you discern the differences when evaluating claims: actively questioning statements, rather than blindly accepting them

essential attitudes include being open-minded and being skeptical

  • critical thinkers are open to new information, ideas, and claims
  • however, their open-minded attitude is tempered by a healthy sense of scientific skepticism.

a critical thinker consistently asks, "What evidence supports this claim?"

Four steps to determine the validity of a claim:

  1. Identify the claim Can the claim be tested scientifically or is it too vague?
    • Try to restate the claim in the form of a hypothesis.
      • What are the variables?
        • How could they be defined and measured?
  2. Evaluate the Evidence Is there empircal evidence?
    • Are the studies descriptive or expiremental. Correlation is NOT causation.
    • Testimonials are NOT evidence
  3. Consider alternative explanations
    • especially with unusual claims or those that contradict accepted scientific theories
    • consider extraneous variables, results due to chance, the placebo effect, etc.
  4. Consider the source of the research or claim
    • has the research been published in peer-reviewed journals?
    • consider the resercher's motives and/or financial ties

Psychologists have conducted thousands of research studies on learning and memory. We can apply the findings to study smarter, not harder

Strategies supported by research:

  • Focus your attention
    • Psychological research on attention reveals that focusing our attention enhances our learning. To study effectively, avoid multitasking and distractions. Consider setting a timer to study uninterrupted for 30 minutes.
  • Engage your mind: be an active reader
    • Being an active reader helps absorb information effectively. Rather than passively reading, interact with the material by taking notes, asking questions, and making connections. Highlighting can be helpful, but only if done properly. Excessive highlighting can be a problem.
  • In the classroom, take notes by hand, not on your laptop
    • Psychological research suggests that taking notes by hand, instead of on a laptop, improves retention and understanding. Handwriting fosters deeper ngagement with the material and a laptop offers many potential distractions. Consider handwriting your otes.
  • Practice retrieval: use the testing effect to your advantage
    • The testing effect demonstrates that quizzing yourself reinforces learning and improves long-term retention. It also helps with the management of test anxiety.
  • Space out your study time: distributed studyis better than massed study
    • Distributed study time, spaced over intervals, is more effective than massed study sessions (AKA cramming). This is especially important for the long-term retention of the material.
