Spearman's Theory

Charles Spearman believed that a single factor (g) underlies many different kinds of mental abilities

  • g factor or general intelligence: the notion of a genreal intelligence factor that is responsible for a person's overall performance on tests of mental ability
  • Spearman recognized that individuals could excel in specific areas, but his overall statistical analysis found that people who scored highly on one type of test usually scored highly on others. Similarly, peole who had low scored in one testing area, typically had lower scores on other areas
  • psychologists who agree with Spearman believe that intelligence scan be accuurately expressed by a single number, such as an IQ score

Part 1: Defining Intelligence

Different Perspectives on Intelligence

defined as the global capacity to think rationally, act purposefully, and deal effectively with the environment

There is considerable disagreement about the nature of intelligence, incuding how it should best be defined

  • is intelligence a single, general ability, or is it a cluster of different mental abilities?
  • should the definition of intelligence be restricted to mental abilities measured by IQ and other intelligence tests, or should intelligence be defined more broadly?

Gardner's Theory

Howard Gardner described "multiple intelligences" and that mental abilities are independent of each other and cannot be accurately reflected in a single measure of intelligence

  • according to Gardner, intelligence must be defined within a cultural context
    • an intelligence is the ability to solve problems, or to create products that are valued within a cultural context
  • Gardner defined 8 distinct, independent intelligences:
    • linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, interpersonal, intrapersonal, bodily-kinesthetic, naturalist
  • research has not generally supported Gardner's theory

Sternberg's Theory

Robert Sternberg agreed with Gardner's premise that intelligence cannot be adequately measured by standard IQ tests. However, he disagreed with Gardner's multiple, independent intelligences, describing some as specialized talents.

Robert emphasized both the universal aspects of intelligent behavior and the importance of adapting to a particular social and cultural environment

  • proposed the triarchic theory of intelligence, 3 distinct forms of successful intelligence:
    • Analytic: learning how to solve problems
    • Cretive: ability to deal with novel situations
    • Practical: the ability to adapt to the environment; street smarts

Carroll Three-Stratum model and the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) Theory

several models integrate the notion of general intelligence with the concept of multiple intelligences. The CHC model is the msot widely accepted theory of intelligence and incorporates the Caroll Three-Stratum model. The combined models break intelligence into three layers or strata:

  1. broadest and represents general intelligence, or g
  2. broad abilities, such as processing speed, quantitative knowledge, reading and writing
  3. narrow abilities that fall under the category of each broad ability

this type of model bridges the gap between intelligence researchers and those who work in the field, such as school psychologists

Changing Ideas of Intelligence

the understanding of intelligence and attempts to measure it have changed over time:

  1. initially, scores were used to predict something (e.g., how someone would do in school or in a particular job)
  2. then, scored were used as a direct measure of someone's abilities
  3. now, measures quantify a range od specific cognitive abilities; these cognitive abilities are shaped by culture and social interactions (they are not innate)

Ackerman suggest that what a successful adult is expected to be good at has changed over the years

  • ex: the internet has changed the way we access and use info

the history of intelligence testing, including the historical use and misuse in the context of fairness

The History of Intelligence Tests

In the early 1900s, the French government passed a law requiring all children to attend school

Alfred Binet

a French psychologist who devised a series of tests to measure different mental abilities with the goal of identifying children who could benefit from special help

  • he focused on basic mental abilities, such as memory, attention, and understanding similarities and differences
  • he invented the concept of mental age (mental level is expressed in terms of the average abilities of a given age group)
  • he did not believe that he was measuring an inborn or permanent level of intelligence
  • Binet's work was embraced by supporters of the eugenics movement
    • Eugenics: the dangerous belief that human genetics can be improved by systematically preventing members of certain groups thought to be inferior (often based on race) from breeding
      • Institutional screening: American psychologist and eugenicist, Henry Goddard, translated Binet's work into Enlgish and used the test to justify institutionalizing some children as "feeble-minded"
      • Immigration: immigrants were screened at Ellis Island. The impact of language and cultural differences was not considered, and false generalizations were made about different nationalities and races

Lewis Terman

an American psychologist who translated and adapted the Binet-Simon intellifence test for use in the United States

  • the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale was first published in 1916
  • Terman developed the concept of intelligence quotient (IQ) by dividing the child's mental age by chronological age and multiplyinf the result by 100
    • IQ = (MA/CA)*100
    • Intelligence Quotient: a measure of general intelligence derviced by comparing an individual's score with the scores of others in the same age group

World War One

the U.S. military needed to qiockly screen 2 million amry recruits

  • Army Alpha test: was administered in writing
  • Army Beta test: was administered orally to recruits and draftees who could not read

at the end of the war, these group intelligence tests were adapted for civilian use. They were widely used and misused

  • there was now a belief that the IQ score was a fixed, inborn characteristic that was resistant to change

David Wechsler

an American psyschologist who developed the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS), which was first published in 1955. It had two advantages over the Stanford-Binet

  • it was specifically designed for adults, rather than for children
  • it included 11 subtests measuring different abilities and generated an overall verbal score, and overall performance score, and an IQ
    • IQ is calculated by comparing an individual's score with scores of others in the same general age group
    • the average score for a particular age group was statistically fixed at 100. Two-thirds of all scores fall between 85 and 115 - the range considered to indicate "normal" or "average" intelligence
  • revised in 1981, 1997, 2008, 2024, it's the most commonly administered intelligence test

Measuring the Contributions of Nature and Nurture

virtually all psychologists agree that both heredity and environment are important in determining intelligence level, the disagreement in the balance of each. Both genes and environment contribute, but the relationship is compex

  • genes are NOT an unchanging, permanent blueprint for development
  • environmental factors influence which genes are expressed
  • many genes affect the range of intellectual potential

The heredity-environment issue has been explored by comparing the IQ scored of individuals who are genetically related to different degrees:

  • Identical twins share exactly the same genes because they developed from a single fertilized egg that split into two
    • any dissimilarities between them are attributed to environment factors, rather than hereditary differences
  • Fraternal twins: are like any other pair of siblings because they develop from two different fertilized eggs

The closer the genetic relationship, the more similar the IQ scores. Researchers have scientifically estimated heritability, which is the percentage of variation within a given population that is due to heredity

  • currently accepted heritability estimate is ~50% of the difference in IQ scores within a given population is due to genetic factors
  • applies only to groups - not to specific individuals

Environmental influences - identical twins raised in different homes have different IQ scores

it is possible to estimate the degree of difference within a specific group that's due to heredity, but not the difference between groups that due to heredity

  • unless the environmental conditions of two groups are virtually indentical, it is impossible to estimate the overall genetic differences between two groups

other evidence fro the importance of the environment in determining IQ scores:

  • improvement in average IQ scores has occured in several cultures and countries during the past few generations (Flynn effect)
  • a total of 30 nations have shown significant gains in average IQ scores
  • Average IQ score in the US has also steadily increased over the past century
  • such changes in a population can be accounte for only by environmental changes (time too short for genetically influenced changed)

Effects of Social Discrimination

the effect of social discrimination on intelligence test scores has been shown in many research studies, including cross-cultural studies

example: overt discrimination against the estimated 1 million Buraku people is technically illegal in Japan, yet they remain the poorest group in Japan, and discrimination persists in employment, marraige, and other areas (Ikeda, 2001). Burakumin of Japan are not racially different from other Japanese, but have suffered from generations of discrimination.

  • their average IQ scores are ~10-15 points below those of mainstream Japanese
  • when Burakumin families immigrate to the United States, the children do just as well in school - and on IQ tests - as any other Japanese Americans

in many other cultures, a similar gap in IQ scores exists between the discriminated-against minority, and the dominant group

stereotypes can be maniuplated to affect tets performance. According to psychologist Claude Steele, stereotype threat occurs when members of a particular group fear that they will be evaluated in terms of a negative stereotype about their group

  • this fear creates anxiety and doubt and lowers performance
  • numerous studies show that performance on tests is suprisingly susceptible to stereotype threat

Cultural Bias

Culture and cultural bias in test construction can affect performance. Tests reflect the culture in which they are developed

  • if standardized intelligence tests reflect White, middle-class cultural knowledge and values, minority-group members might do poorly on the tests, not because of lower intelligence, but because of unfamiliarity with teh White, middle-class culture
  • a test will tend to favor the people from the culture in which it was developed

cultural differences may also be involved in test-taking behavior

  • people from different cultural backgrounds may use strategies that are different from those required on standard intelligence tests
  • cultural factors, such as motivation, attitudes toward test taking, and previous experiences with tests can affect performance and scores on tests
