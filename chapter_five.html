Part 1: Early Research on Learning - Classical Conditioning

Early Perspective of Psychology that Studied the Fundamental Principles of Learning

Learning is a process that produces a relatively enduring change in behavior or knowledge as a result of past experience

Conditioningis a basic form of learning. It is the process of learning associations between environmental events and behavioral responses. There are two forms:

  • Classical Conditioning
  • Operant Conditioning

Ivan Pavlov, Russian physiologist, pioneered work that set the foundation for the scientific study of learning. The process of conditioning he discovered was the first to be extensively studied in psychology.

  • Pavolv's work inspired one of the most influential early perspectives in pyschology:
    • Behaviorism: founded and championed by John Watson, was an early perspective that emphasized the study of observable behaviors, especially as they pertain to the process of learning

The Principles of Classical Conditioning

Pavlov

  • awarded a Nobel Prize for his work on digestion
  • While studying digestion in dogs, he stumbled upon the concept of classical conditioning
  • He abandoned his research on digestion and decided to study this phenomenon scientifically

Classical Conditioning

also known as Pavlovian conditioning, the basic learning process that involves repeatedly pairing a neutral stimulus with a response-producing stimulus until the neutral stimulus elicits the same response

Reflex: a relatively simple, unlearned behavior, governed by the nervous system that occurs automatically when the appropriate stimulus is presented

Elements of Classic Conditioning:

  • Neutral Stimulus: a stimulus that does not naturally elicit the reflexive response
  • Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS): natural stimulus that reflexively elicits a response without the need for prior learning
  • Unconditioned Response (UCR): unlearned, reflexive response that is elicited by an unconditioned stimulus
  • Conditioned Stimulus (CS): formerly neutral stimulus that acquires the capacity to elicit a reflexive response
  • Conditioned Response (CR): learned, reflexive response to a conditioned (previously neutral) stimulus

Palov discovered factors that could strengthen the association between the stimuli and procude a stronger conditioned response (CR)

  • paiting the stimuli (NS/CS with the US) frequently
  • timing (1/2 to a few seconds) between the stimuli
  • NS/CS should immediately precede the UCS

Pavolv also studied the impact of stimulus characteristics

  • Stimulus Generalization: the occurence of a learned response not only to the original conditioned stimulus, but to other, similar stimuli as well
  • Stimulus Discrimination: the occurence of a learned resposne to specific stimulus, but not to other, similar stimuli
    Pavolv investigated whether the learned association could be broken.
  • Extinction: gradual weakening and apparent disappearance of a conditioned behavior; occurs when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS
  • Spontaneous Recovery: reappearance of a previously extenguished CR after a period of time without exposur to CS
    • provides support for the idea that a conditioned response that is extinguished is not unlearned or completely eliminated

Turner

  • referred to as the first African American psychologist
  • was orignally trained as a zoologist

    • used classical condition techniques to demonstrate that moths could hear
    • used learning techniques to prove that bees could see color
  • long ignored and denied a university position, yet published groundbreaking academic work (von Frisch technique)

Watson

  • John Watson
  • adovocated for the scientific study of objectively observed behavior
  • believed all human behavior is a result of conditioning and learning
  • believed that human emotions could be thought of as reflexive responses involving muscles and glands
  • conducted controversial expirement called the "Case of Little Albert"

Case of Little Albert

In 1920, Watson and graduate student, Rayner, set out to demonstrate that classical conditioning could be used to deliberately established a conditioned emotional esponse in a human subject

  1. 9-month-old Albert showed no fear when presented with a tame white rat (neutral stimulus), a rabbit, a dog, and a monkey
  2. When a steel bar was banged behind his head (unconditioned stimulus), Albert respomded with fear (unconditioned response).
  3. After seven pairings of the loud noise and the white rat, the white rat alone triggered extreme fear (conditioned response). The white rat became a conditioned stimulus because it could trigger the fear response without the loud noise
  4. Stimuus generalization also occurred: Little Albert was now afraid of other furry animals and a variety of fuzzy objects

Criticisms: the study was not carefully designed or conducted, and the researchers made no effort to extinguish the fear.

Part 2: Operant Conditioning

The Principles of Operant Conditioning

B.F. Skinner, an American psychologist, searched for the "lawful processes" that would explain "order in behavior". He was a behaviorist. He believed that psychology should restrict itself by studying only phenomena that could be objectively measured and verified — only outwardly observable behavior and environment events.

Skinner acknowledged the importance of classical conditioning for reflective responses, but he wanted to study new behaviors that are actively emitted

  • he coined the term operant to describe any "active behavior that operates upon the environment to generate consequences"
  • he conducted research with small animals (e.g., rat, pigeon) in a specially designed "operant chamber"
  • Skinner systematically manipulated the consequences of voluntary behaviors (operants)

Skinner objectively studied that principles of operant conditioning

  • the basic learning process that involves changing the probability that a response will be repeated by manipulating the consequence of that response
  • consequences may take the form of Reinforcement or punishment:
    • Reinforcement: a consequence that increases the likelihood of the behavior being repeated
    • Punishment: a consequence that decreases the likelihood of that behavior being repeated
  • the organism learns to associate specific environment conditions in which a particular behavior will receive a consequence. This type of environmental cue becomes a discriminative stimulus
    • ex: toddler throws a trantrum with one parent and not another, or at one place vs at home

Types of Reinforcement

a consequence that increases the likelihood of the behavior being repeated. two types:

  • Positive Reinforcement: response is followed by the addition of a reinforcing stimulus
    • increases the likelihood that the response will be repeated in similar situations
  • Negative Reinforcement: response results in the removal of, avoidance of, or escape from an aversive stimulus
    • increases the likelihood that the response will be repeated in similar situations

Two forms of reinforcing stimuli:

  • Primary Reinforcer: stimulus or event that is naturally or inherently reinforcing for a given species (e.g., food, water, or other biological necessities)
  • Conditioned (secondary) Reinforcer: stimulus or event that has acquired reinforcing value by being associated with a primary reinforcer (e.g., money, awards, frequent-flyer points)

Types of Punishment

the presentation of a stimulus or event following a behavior that acts to decrease the likelihood of the behavior being repeated (many confuse punishment and negative reinforcement). Two types:

  • Positive Punishment: punishment by application; a situation in which an operant is followed by the presentation or addition of an aversive stimulus
  • Negative Punishment: punishment by removal; a situation in which an operant is followed by the removal or subtraction of a reinforcing stimulus

factors that influence in the effectiveness of punishment:

  • punishment is more effective when the consequence immediately follows the operant. Any delay weakens the conditioning

    • punishment is more effective when the consequence consistently follows the operant. Inconsistency weakens the conditioning

punishment has several drawbacks:

  • it doesn't teach a more appropriate response
  • it may have undesirable results, such as complete passivity, fear, anxiety, or hostility
  • its effects are likely to be temporary

noting the drawbacks of punishment, Skinner advoated for the use of reinforcement for behavior change. Alternatives to punishment:

  • reinforce an incompatible behavior
  • stop reinforcing the problem behavior (extinction)
  • reinforce the nonoccurence of the problem behavior
  • remove the opportunity to obtain positive reinforcement (time-out from positive reinforcement)

Shaping

in operant conditioning, sometimes you cannot afford to wait for a correct response to provide a reinforcing consequence

  • complex behaviors may not be performed spontaneously
  • training must take a more gradual, step-by-step approach

Shaping: selectively reinforcing successively closer approximations of goal behavior until goal behavior is displayed; irrelevant responses are ignored

  • Skinner believed that shaping could explain how people acquire several skills and abilities

Continuous VS Partial Reinforcement

Skinner orginally used continuous reinforcement: every occurence of a particular response is reinforced

  • he found continuous reinforcement to be the fastest way to strengthen (increase the frequency of) a response

Running out of food for his rats, Skinner stratched out the pellets by not rewarding every trial and discovered the partial reinforcement effect:

  • behaviors that are conditioned using partial reinforcement are more resistant to extinction than behaviors that are conditioned using continuous reinforcement

Extinction:: in operant conditioning, a phenomenon that occurs when a learned response no longer results in reinforcement, and the likelihood of the behavior being repeated gradually declines

Part 3: Cognitive Approaches and Applications

Cognitive Approaches to Learning

Observational Learning: learning that occurs through observing the actions of others

Albert Bandura contends that most human behavior is acquired through observational learning. He demonstrated the influence of observational learning in a series of experiments conducted in the early 1960s.

One of these studies was described in the textbook:

Part 1: 4-year-old children were randomly assigned to view a version of a film. . .
All films were identical (showing an adult beating a "Bobo doll") except for the ending . . .
- Version 1: the adult was rewarded with soda and candy
- Version 2: the adult was punished
- Version 3: the were no consequences
Part 2: each child was placed alone in a room with a variety of toys (including Bobo)
- children were observed though a one-way window
Results: the children who observed thepunishing consequences were much less likely to imitate the aggressive behavior than the other groups

Bandura's reseach studied also showed that observing people wh are rewarded for their behavior are likely to be imitated

  • according to Bandura, experiencing reinforcement is not essential for learning to occur. Rather, the expectation of reinforcement affects the performance of what has been learned

4 Cognitive processes interact to determine whether imitation will occur:

  1. Paying attention to the other person's behavior
  2. Forming and storing mental representations of the behavior to be imitated
  3. Transforming this mental representation into actions you are capable of reproducing
  4. Being motivated to imitate the behavior by some expectation that doing so will produce reinforcement or reward

Bandura's research showed many factors increase the likelihood of imitation:

  • people who are rewarded for their behavior
  • warm, nurturing people
  • people who have control over you or have the power to influence your life
  • people who are similar to you in terms of age, sex, and interests
  • people you perceive as having higher social status
  • when the task to be imitated is not extremely easy or difficult
  • if you lack confidence in your own abilities in a particular situation
  • if the situation is unfamiliar or ambigious
  • if you've been rewarded for imitating the same behavior in the past

Applications of Learning Theories in Real Life

Learning Theories:

  • Classical Conditioning
    • Type of behavior: reflexive, involuntary behaviors
    • Source of behavior: elicited by stimulus
    • Basis of learning: associating two stimuli: CS + UCS
    • Responses conditioned: physiological and emotional responses
    • Extinction process: conditioned response decreases when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS
  • Operant Conditioning
    • Type of behavior: non-reflexive, voluntary behaviors
    • Source of behavior: emitted by organism
    • Basis of learning: associating a response and the consequence that follows it
    • Responses conditioned: active behaviors that operate on the environment
    • Extinction process: responding decreases with elimination of reinforcing consequences

The behaviorists studing classical conditioning and operant conditioning dismissed cognitive factors. They emphasizes direct experience

Bandura's social learning theory contends that most human behavior is aquired through observation learning. Direct experiences with stimuli and consequences are not necessary. Congnitive factors are emphasized through watching and processing of information about the actions of others and the observed consequences of those action.

Application of Classical Conditioning

Watson, Classical Conditioning, and Advertising

  • John Watson left academia and joined advertising agency. He was a pioneer in applying classical conditioning principles to advertising.
    • Ex: ads for Johnson and Johnson baby powder tried to manipulate the anxieties of new mothers
  • Associating advertising images with emotional repsonses has been shown by research to be effective in influencing attitudes toward products or brands

Virtually any automatic response can become classically conditioned. It has been shown to affect drug responses

  • regular use of a drug may produce a "placebo response", where the user associates sight, smell, taste with the drug effect
    • a regular coffee drinker may feel more alrt after drinking decaf due to the association of the taste and smell of coffee with caffeine

Applications of Operant Conditioning

  • specialized training of animals: A variety of species are trained to assist humans in tasks and therapeutic roles
  • behavior modification: the application of learning principles to help people develop more effective or adaptive behaviors. The systematic use of reinforcement and shaping results in the increased occurence of desirable behaviors
    • has been used for improving worker performance, increasing social skills, and decreasing self-destructive behaviors in people with autism spectrum disorders, decreasing problem behaviors in school children, etc.
  • gamification: turning daily life into a kind of virtual reality game, in which "points" or other conditioned reinforcers are awarded to reward healthy and productive behaviors

Applications of Obervational Learning

  • Media Violence and Aggresion: research suggests that exposure to media violence can cause short-term increases in laboratory measures of aggressive thoughts and behavior; numerous correlational studies show a link between exposure to violent media and aggressive behavior
    • however, most research studies are correlational, which cannot estblish cause and effect relationships. expiremental studies rely on artificial conditions and may not generalize well to real-life situations
    • reviewing the accumulation of decades of research evidence, the American Psychological Association Task Force Assessment of Violent Video Games concluded that playing violent video games is related to increased aggressive behavior as wella s reduced empathy toward others, although it did not find evidence for a link with criminal behavior (Calvert et al., 2017)
    • psychologists generally agree that violence is complex, with no single cause. Additionally, some viewers are highly susceptible to the negative effects of media violence
  • Education-Entertainment Programs: designed to fulfill the optimal conditions for observational learning to occur (Bandura, 2002), the programs show people facing everyday problems and modeling functional strategies and skills
    • ex: Mexican TV exec, Migeul Sabido, produced a long-running serial drama that promoted literacy among adults. Enrollment in literacy groups was approximately 90,000 before the series, but the enrollment jumped to 840,000 when the series aired. Research studies have confirmed the highly successful impact of these dramas
