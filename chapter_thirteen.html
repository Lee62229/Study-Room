Part 1: Defining Stress

stress and how it is a psychophysiological reaction

Stress: negative emotional state occuring in response to events that are perceived as taxing or exceeding a person's resources or ability to cope

Perception is important

Cognitive Appraisal Model of Stress

Developed by Richard Lazarus, a model of stress that emphasizes the role of an individual's evaluation (appraisal) of events and situations, and the resources available to deal with the event of sitaution

Health Psychology

the branch of psychology that studies how biological, behavioral, and social factors influence health, illness, medical treatment, and health-related behaviors

health psychologists are guided by the biopsychosocial model, which is the belief that physical health and illness are determined by the complex interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors

Physical Effects of Stress: The Mind-Body Connection

stress can indirectly affect a person's health

  • prompting behaviors that jeopardize physical well-being, such as not eating or sleeping properly

stress can directly affect physical health

  • altering body functions, leading to physical symptoms, illness, or disease
  • acute stress and prolonged stress activate different endocrine pathways

Stress and the Endocrine System

Acute Stress initiates the Fight-or-Flight response:

  • a rapidly occurring chain of internal physical reactions that prepare people either to fight or take flight from an immediate threat
    • first described by American physiologist Walter Cannon
  • involves the sympathetic nervous system and endocrine system:
    • the hypothalamus and lower brain structures activate the sympathetic nervous system, which stimulates the adrenal medulla to secrete hormones called catecholamines (including adrenaline and nonadrenaline) which cause rapid physiological arousal

Hans Selye studied the effect of prolonged stress

  • he exposed rates to different types of prolonged stress
  • regardless of the type of stress, the pattern of physical response was the same
    • adrenal glands became enlarged
    • stomach ulcers and weight loss occured
    • immune system (thymus and lymph) glands shrank
  • Selye found that prolonged stress activates a second endocrine pathway involving the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and andrenal cortext
    • stress causes the pituitary to release ACTH
    • ACTH causes adrenals to release corticosteroids (cortisol)
      • in the shortrun, the corticosteroids help protect the body against harm (reduce inflammation and enhance muscles)
      • continued high levels of corticosteroids can lower immunity and increase susceptibility to illness

General Adaptation Syndrome: Hans Slye's term for the three-stage progression of physical changes that occur when an organism is exposed to intense and prolonged stress

  1. Alarm
    • intense arousal
    • mobilization of physical resources (catecholamines)
  2. Resistance
    • body actively tries to resist or adjust
    • arousal is less than in the "alarm" stage, but higher than normal
  3. Exhaustion
    • symptoms of the alarm stage reappear, only now irreversibly
    • energy reserves depleted, adaptation breaks down, leading to exhaustion, physical disorders, and potentially death

Stress and the Immune System

Immune Systems:

  • compromises several organs including bone marrow, the spleen, and lymph nodes
  • produces lymphocytes: specialized white blood cells that fight bacteria, viruses, and other foreign invaders

Psychoneuroimmunology is an interdisciplinary field that studies the interconnections among psychological processes, nervous system, and immune system

immune - endocrine - nervous system interactions:

  • central nervous system and the immune system are directly linked via sympathetic nervous system
  • surfaces of lymphocytes contain receptor sites for neurotransmitters and hormones, including catecholamines and cortisol
  • lymphocytes themselves produce neurotransmitters and hormons

highly stressful events and common stresses are associated with reduced immune system functioning

Stress and wound healing: a study by Marucha (1998) administered 2 puncture wounds to the mouths of dental students. One occured days before a major exam, and one occured during summer break. The wounds prior to the exam took approximately 3 days longer to heal

Stress and the common cold: a series of carefully controlled studies by Cohen and colleagues (2006; 2012) showed that people experiencing high levels of stress were more susceptible to infectin with a cold virus than people who were under low stress

  • those who experience chronic stressors were most susceptible

The relationship between stress and illness is complex:

  • prolonged, chronic stress impairs immune functioning, but acute stress can actually enhance it
  • physical health is affected by many factors, including heredity, nutrition, health-related habits, and access to medical care
  • some people are more vulnerable to the negative effects of stress than others

Part 2: Sources of Stress

Stressors

events or situations that are perceived as harmful, threatening, or challenging

  • Life events and change
    any event that requires a person to change or adjust their lifestyle will produce significant stress (whether the event is positive or negative, planned or unexpected)
    • Holmes and Rahe (1967) developed the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS) assigning point values for various life events
      • asked people to identify all the events that happened to them in the last year
      • found that people with scores greater than 150 ahs an increased rate of physical and psychological illness
      • criticisms
        • some research has shown that negative life events have a more adverse effect than positive events
        • unexpected events that bring uncertainty may be more stressful for some
        • this approach assumes that a given life event will have the same impact on everyone by assigning a specific number of "life changing units". It does not ocnsider an individual's cognitive appraisal
    • revised and updated life event scales
      • some consider influences of gender, marital status, and other individual characteristics
      • there are scales developed for specific groups (e.g., Academic Stress Measire for university students)
      • some include cross-cultural factors such as race and ethnicity
  • traumatic events
    events or situations that are negative, severe, and far beyond our normal expectations for everyday life or life events
    • when traumas are intense or repeated, some psychologically vulnerable people may develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)
      • symptoms include recurring, intrusive recollections as well as emotional numbness, symptoms of anxiety, sleep disturbances, and irritability
    • a large survey of college students found that 85% reported having been exposed to a traumatic event during their lifetime
      • most people recover from traumatic evnts without ever developing PTSD
      • most people are remarkably resilient
        • Resilience: the ability to cope with stress and adversity, and to rebound after negative experiences
    • Seery and others (2010) conducted a large-scale longitudinal study measuring cumulative adversity (the total amount of negative events experienced over a lifetime) and health outcomes
      • high and low levels of cumulative adversity were associated with poor health outcomes
      • experiencing some stress was healthier than experiencing no stress at all
      • people who have had to cope with a moderate level of adversity develop resilience
  • daily hassles
    everyday minor events that annoy and upset people
    • frequency of daily hassles and the tendency to react negatively to them are linked to decreased well-being , unhealthy behaviors, and increased illness (mental and physical)
    • some research has found that the numbr of daily hassles is a better predictor of physical illness and symptoms than the numbr of major life events experienced
      • the effect of daily stressors are cumulative
    • gender differences
      • interpersonal conflict is the most common source of daily stress for both men and women
      • women are more likely to report daily stressors that are associated with friends and family
      • men are more likely to feel hassled by stressors that are school-related or work-related
  • work stress
    burnout is an unhealthy condition caused by chronic, prolonged work stress; it is characterized by exhaustion, cynicism, and a sense of failure or inadequacy
    • can be caused by
      • work overload, when the demands of the job exceed the worker's ability to meet them
      • a lack of control
    • can be avoided by
      • when a sense of community exists in the workplace
      • by job crafting: proactively changing demands of the job, or personal resources to meet those demands
  • social and cultural sources of stress

Social and Cultural Sources of Stress

people in less privileged groups often live under difficult or unpleasant conditions and experience chronic stress

  • crowding, crime, poverty, and substandard housing

chronic stress is associated with lower socioeconomic status (income, education, and occupation); people in lower SES groups:

  • may experience more negaticve life events and daily hassles
  • have fewer resources to help them cope
  • in addition, personal perception of low social status is associated with poorer physicl health

Racism and discrimination are another important source of chronic stress

  • subtle instances of racism, called microaggressions, contribute to chronic stress
    • in one survey, investigators interested in the relationship between racism and chronic stress found that 3/4 of Black adolescents reported being treated as incompetent or dangerous, or both, because of thier race
  • experiencing racism can increase the risk of stress-related healt problems, such as hypertension

Acculturative stress resuts from the pressure of adapting to a new culture

  • two questions people face when entering a new culture:
    1. Should I seek positive relations with the dominant society?
    2. Is my original culture identity of valie to me, and should I try to maintain it?
  • The Four Acculturation Strategies
    • Integration yes to both questions - low stress
      the individual maintains their original cultural identity while also seeking positive interactions and involvement with the dominant society
    • Assimilation yes to Question 1, no to Question 2 - moderate stress
      the individual gives up their original cultural identity to fullt adopt the values and traditions of the dominant society
    • Separation no to Question 1, yes to Question 2 - high stress
      the individual holds onto their original cultural identity, but avoids or rejects involvement with the dominant society
    • Marginalization no to both - greatest stress
      the individual neither maintains their original cultural identity, nor seeks involvement with teh dominant society, often resulting in cultural loss and social isolation

Part 3: Supporting Health and Managing Stress

Behaviors and Attitudes that Promote Health

Individual Factors

can modify the stress response and promote physical health

  • personal control: sense of control over a stress-producing event
    • reduces the impact of stressors
    • decreases feelings of depession and anxiety
    • Rodin and Langer (1977)
      • nursing-home residents who had opportunities to make choices (high control) were more active, alert, and healthier than those who did not (low control)
      • twice as many low-control residents passed away
    • perception of control must be realistic to be adaptive
    • personal control is more valued in individualistic cultures
  • optimistic explanatory style
    • Martin Seligman (1990, 1992) studied how people characteristically explain their defeats and failures
      • potimistic explanatory style: external, unstable, specific explanations for negative events
      • Pessimistic explanatory style: interna;. stable, global explanations for negative events
    • explanatory style for negative events is relatively stable across the lifespan; it is linked to health consequences
      • one study showed that explanatory style in young adulthood predicted physical health decades later
      • another study found that the first year law students who has an optimistic explanatory style had better immune system indicators
  • positive emotions: associated with better health
    • increased resistance to infection, decreased illnesses, fewer reports of illness symptoms, less pain, and increased longevity
    • chronic negative emotions have a strong link with poor health
      • people who are habitually anxious, depressed, angry, or hostile are more likely to develop a chronic disease, such as arthritis or heart disease
    • Type A behavior pattern: time urgency, hostility, competitiveness
    • Type B Behavior pattern: more relaxed, laid-back approach to life
      • cardiologists Friedman and Rosenman conducted the original research
        • interviewed and classified over 3,000 middle-aged, healthy men as Type A or Type B
        • tracked them for 8 years
        • type A men were 2x more likely to develop heart disease
        • later research: key component = hostility
          • the tendency to feel anger, annoyance, resentment, and contempt
          • hostile people have more intense reactions to stressors, including greater increases in blood pressure and heart rate
          • their attitudes and behaviors create more stressful situations; they experience more frequent life events and hassles

Social Factors

social support: the resources provided by other people in times of need; soocial support has three broad categories: emotional, tangible, and informational:

  • emotional support: includes expresions of concern, empathy, and positive regard
  • Tangible support: involves direct assistance, such as providing transportation, lending money, or helping with meals, childcare, or household tasks
  • Information support: is provided when people offer helpful suggestions, advice, or possible resources

postive effect of diverse social networks (having different types of relationships (e.g., marriage, close with family, friends, neighbors, member in clubs, organizations, religious groups, etc.)):

  • greater resistance to upper respiratory infections
  • lower incidence of stroke and cardiovascular disease among women in a high-risk group
  • lower indidence of dementia and cogniticve loss in old age

research outcomes related to social isolation (the objective state of not having social contact and social relationships) and loneliness (an unpleasant emotional state in which a person desires more or better social contact and social relationships):

  • a study of almost 25,00 adults in Denmark found link between both loneliness and social isolation and chronic disease, including cardiovascular disease and cancer
  • social isolation is also linked to dementia and cognitive loss in old age
  • socially isolated people have poorer health and higher death rates
  • social isolation and loneliness seems to be as potent a health risk as smoking, obesity, alcohol abuse, and physical inactivity

how social support benefits health

  • can modify our appraisal of a stressor's significance
  • seems to decrease the intensity of physical reactions to a stressor
  • makes people less likely to experience negative emotions
  • negative impacts:
    • negative interactions can create psychological distress
    • unwanted or inappropriate social support can increase stress

Strategies to Deal with Stress

Coping: behavioral and cognitive reponses used to deal with stressors; efforts to change circumstances, or our interpretation of them to make them more favorable and less threatening

effective coping is flexibale and adaptive

  • dynamic and complex process
  • realistically evaluating the situation and determining what can be done to minimize the impact of the stressor
  • dealing with the emotional aspects of the situation

Problem-Focused Coping

aimed at managing or changing the stressor; is most effective when control can be exercised; example of problem-focused coping:

  • Planful problem solving
    • analyzes the situation rationally
    • identifies potential solutions
    • implements solutions
  • Confrontive coping
    • tackles the problem "head on"
    • may use aggressive or risky tactics
    • better with direct and assertive tactics than hostile

Emotion-Focused Coping

directs effort toward relieving or regulating emotional impact of a stressful situation; often occurs when people believe nothing can be done to alter the situation; examples of emotion-focused coping:

  • Escape-Avoidance: shifts the attention away from the stressor and toward other activities
    • can be problematic when a stressor requires immediate attention
  • Seeking social support: involves turning to other people
  • Distancing: acknowledges the stressor, but tried to minimize its emotional impact
    • may emotionally distance a stressor by discussing it in a detached or intellectual way
    • may use humor
  • Denial: a refusal to acknowledge that the problem even exists
  • Positive Reappraisal: tries to create positive meaning by focusing on personal growth
    • the most constructive emotion-focused strategy
  • Positive religious coping: includes seeking comfort or reassurance in prayer, a religious ocmmunity, or believing your personal experience is spiritually meaningful
  • Negative religious coping: when individuals become angry and question their religious beliefs or feel that they are being punsihed
    • this tends to increase distress and decrease well-being

Culture and Coping Strategies

  • Individualistic cultures
    • emphasize personal autonomy and personal responsibility in dealing with problems
    • emphasize the importance and value of exerting control over circumstances
    • are less likely to seek social support in stressful situations than are members of collectivistic cultures
    • favor problem-focused strategies, such as confrontive coping and planful problem-solving
  • Collectivistic Cultures
    • are oriented toward social group, family, or community, and toward seeking help with problems
    • place a greater emphasis on controlling personal reactions
    • are more likely to rely on emotional coping strategies than people in individualistic cultures
