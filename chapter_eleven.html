Part 1: Major Theoretical Approaches

Major Theoretical Approaches to Personality

The Psychoanalytic Perspective

Psychoanalysis is the theory of personality that emphasizes unconscious determinants of behavior, sexual and aggressive instinctual drives, and the enduring effects of eary childhood experiences on later personality development.

Sigmund Freud, the founder of psychoanalysis, was one of the most influential figures of the twentieth century. Freud was a physician who developed his theory over decades of work with patients in his private practice and through his own self-analysis.
Initially, he used hypnosis to help patients. Then he developed his own technique of free association to help patients uncover forgotten memories.

  • Free association: a psychoanalytical technique in which the patient spontaneously reports all thoughts, feelings, and mental images as they come to mind.

Freud saw personality and behavior as a result of a constant interplay among conflicting psychological forces.
Psychological forces operate at 3 different levels of awareness:

  • conscious
  • preconscious
  • unconscious

The basic structures (processes) of personality are the Id, Ego, and Superego:

  • Id
    completely unconscious, irrational components that seeks immediate gratification
    • does not distinguish reality from fantasy; immune to logic
    • instinctual drives present at birth
    • operates according to pleasure principle
      • Pleasure Principle: motive to obtain pleasure and avoid tension or discomfort; the most fundamental human motive and guiding principle of id
  • Ego
    partially conscious, rational component of personality that regulates thoughts and behavior
    • understands reality and logic
    • is most in touch with the demans of the external world
    • acts as mediator between id and superego
    • operates according to the reality principle
      • Reality Principle: ability to postpone gratification in accordance with demands of external world
    • can repress desires that cannot be met in an acceptable manner
  • Superego
    partly conscious, self-evaluative, moralistic component of personality
    • is formed through internalization of parental and societal rules
    • at age 5 or 6, child develops an internal, parental voice that is partly conscious
    • responsible for guilt; praises and admonishes

Ego Defense Mechanisms

Ego defense mechanisms: largely unconscious distortions of thoughts or perceptions that act to reduce anxiety

If demands of the id or superego threaten to overwhelm the ego, anxiety results. If a realistic solution is not possible, the ego may temporarily reduce anxiety by distorting perceptions of reality through ego defense mechanisms. Short-term use of defense mechanisms can be helpful; long-term use is problematic

  • Repression: the unconscious exclusion of anxiety-provoking thoughts, feelings, and memories from conscious awareness
    Ex: Three years after a traumatic car accident, a person can remember only vague details about the event.
    • considered the most fundamental ego defense mechanism; others are used if repression fails
  • Displacement: redirecting impulses toward a substitute tperson or object that is less threatening than the original source of the emotion
    Ex: angered by the neighbor's hateful comment, a mother spanks her daughter for accidentally spilling her milk
  • Sublimation: a constructive form of displacement where unacceptable urges (often sexual) are rechanneled into productive, socially acceptable activities
    Ex: a graduate student works on her thesis 14 hours a day while her husband is on an extended business trip
  • Rationalization: justifying actions or feelings with socially acceptable explanations to avoid acknowleedging one's true motives or desires
    after being rejected by a prestigious university, a student explains he is glad because he would be happier at a smaller, less competitive school
  • Projection: attributing one's own unacceptable urges, qualities, or thoughts to other people
    Ex: a married woman who is sexually attracted to a co-worker accuses him of flirting with her
  • Reaction Formation: thinking or behaving in a way that is the extreme opposite of one's actual unacceptable urges or impulses
    Ex: threatened by his sexual attraction to girls, an adolescent boy teases and torments adolescent girls
  • Denial: the failure to recognize or acknowledge the existence of information that causes anxiety
    Ex: despite having multiple drinks every night, a man says he is not an alcoholic because he never drinks before 5 pm
  • Regression: retreating to a pattern of behavior characteristic of an earlier, more immature stage of development
    Ex: after her parents' bitter divorce, a 10-year-old girl refuses to sleep alone, crawling into bed with her mother

Evaluating the Psychoanalytic Perspective

  • Strengths:
    • Freudian theory has had profound impact on psychology and on society
    • some important ideas have been substantiated by empirical research
      • much of mental life in unconscious
      • early childhood experiences have a critical influence on interpersonal relationships and psychological adjustment
      • people differ significantly in the degree to which they are able to regulate their impulses, emotions, and thoughts toward adaptive and socially acceptable ends
  • Limitations:
    • inadequacy of evidence
      • Freud's theory relies wholly on data derived from his own relatively small number of patients and from his own self-analysis
      • it is impossible to objectively assess Freud's "data", because he did not take notes during sessions and reported cases consisted of his interpretations
    • lack of testability
      • many psychoanalytic concepts are so vague they are impossible to measure objectively
      • Non Falsifiable: often impossible to disprove; even seemingly contradictory information can be used to support Freud's theory
      • psychoanalysis is better at explaining past behavior than predicting future behavior
    • sexism
      • Freud claimed women are more vain, masochistis, and jealous than men. He also believed that women are more influenced by their emotions and have a lesser ethical and moral sense
      • Freud's theory uses male heterosexual psychology as a prototype

The Humanistic Perspective

Humanistic psychology emerged in opposition to both psychoanalysis and behaviorism

  • championed a "third force" in psychology
  • saw poeple as being innately good
  • focused on the healthy personality and the human potential, self-actualization
  • doubted that laboratory research with rats and pigeons accurately reflected human nature
  • suggested the most important factor in personality is the individual's conscious perception of self (the self-concept)

The two most important contributors to the humanistic perspective were Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow (who developed a theory of motivation)

Psychotherapist, Carl Rogers, developed his personality theory fromhis clinical experiences with his clients. According to Rogers:

  • the most basic human motive is actualizing tendency; other drives are secondary
    • Actualizing tendency: the innate drive to maintain and enhace the human organism
  • when relationsips provide the right conditions, the individual will naturally move toward greater psychological health and growth

Self-Concept: set of perceptions and beliefs that you hold about yourself

  • people are motivated in accordance with their self-concept
  • the self-concept begins to form in early life
  • incresing self-awareness produces a need for positive regard (sense of being loved and valued)

Regard can be conditional or unconditional. Rogers maintained that most parents provide their children with conditional postive regard:

  • Conditional positive regard: the sense that you will be valued and loved, only if you behave in a way that is acceptable to others; conditional love or acceptance
  • Unconditional postive regard: the sense that you will be valued and loved, even if you don't conform to the standards and expectations of others; unconditional love or acceptance

Through consistent experiences of unconditional positive regard, one becomes a psychologically healthy, fully functioning person who has a flexible, constantly evolving self-concept

  • rather than defending against or distorting their own thoughts or feelings, people experience congruence
    • Congruence: one's sense of self is consistent with emotions and experiences
  • on the other hand, if relationships have provided conditional positive regard, problems emerge
    • the self-concept may be an inauthentic or unrealistic, idealized sense of who the person must be to be worthy of acceptance or love
    • a contradicted self-concept produces incongruence
      • Incongruence: the self-concept conflicts with one's actual experience, resulting in a need to defend agaisnt (distort) true feelings and perceptions of experiences
        • the failure to live up to regid and inauthentic or unrealistic self-concept is inevitable. These failures trigger deeper feelings of unworthiness, which trigger defenses
  • Rogers advocated for parents to provide unconditional positive regard, but he did not advocate for permissive parenting
    • he miantained that parents could disapprove of a child's specific behavior without completely rejecting the child's self

Evaluating the Humanistic Perspective

  • Strengths:
    • made contributions to psychotherapy, counseling, education, and parenting
    • Subjective experience and the self-concept has become widely accepted
  • Limitations:
    • difficult to test or validate scientifically
    • based on philosophical assumptions or clinical observations, rather than on empirical research
    • tends to be too optimistic, minimizing some of the more destructive aspects of human nature

The Social Cognitive Perspective

Social Cognitive Theory: Albert Bandura's theory of personality.
Emphasizes the importance of:

  • observational learning
  • conscious cognitive experiences
  • social experiences
  • self-efficacy beliefs
  • reciprocal determinism

Social cognitive thoery emphasizes the social origins of thoughts and actions, but also stresses active (conscious) cognitive processes and the human capacity for self-regulation.

Bandura's research has show the importance of observational learning. We observe the consequences that follow people's actions, the rules and standard that apply to behavior in specific situations, and the ways in which people regulate their own behavior.

Reciprocal Determinism: a model that explains human functioning and personality as caused by the interaction of behavioral, cognitive, and environmental factos.

Collectively, a person's cognitive skills, abilities, and attitudes represent the person's self-system

  • the self-system guides how we perceive, evaluate, and control our behavior in different situations

the most critical elements influencing out self-system are out beliefs of self-efficacy

  • Self-efficacy: the beliefs that people have about their ability to meet the demands of a specific situation

We acquire new behaviors and strengthen our beliefs of self-efficacy in particular situations through observational learning and mastery experiences (succeeding at tasks)

Developing self-efficacy begins in childhood, but it continues as a lifelong process, with each stage of the lifespan presenting new challenges.

Evaluating the Social Cognitive Perspective

  • Strengths:
    • well grounded in empirical, laboratory research
    • offers a developmental understanding of human funcitoning
    • emphasizes the self-regulation understanding of behavior
    • places most of the responsibility for behavior on the person
  • Limitations:
    • laboratory studies examine a limited number of variables and may not reflect the complexity of human interactions in the real world
    • the influences of the unconscious, emotions, adn conflicts are downplayed

The Trait Perspective

The trait perspective focuses on identifying, describing, and measuring individual differences in behavioral dispositions

trait theorists view the person as being a unique combination of personality characteristics or attributes, called traits

  • Trait: a relatively stable, enduring predisposition to behave in a certain way
    • Surface traits: personality characteristics or attributes that can be easily inferred from observable behavior
    • Source traits: the most fundamental dimensions of personality; the broad basic traits that are hypothesized to be universal and relatively few in number
      • a source trait can give rise to many surface traits
      • how many fundamental source traits are there?
        • Raymond Cattell initiallu suggested 16 basic personality factors
        • Hans Eysenck proposed 3 dimensions
        • the five-factor model ("the Big Five") by Robert McCrae and Paul Costa, is the current view of the number of source traits
          • this theory suggests that personality can be reduced to five common traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism

The Big Five

Factors are rated from low to high on a continuum, and are compared to norms based on others of a similar age, determining where they have on each continuum. The pattern of scores across these five factors provdes an overall description of an individual's personality (use "OCEAN" to remember them)

  • Openness to Experience: traditional and gounded to curious and creative
    • down-to-earth vs imaginative
    • conventional, uncreative vs original, creative
    • prefer routine vs prefer variety
  • conscientiousness: organization, dependability, drive
    • lazy vs hardworking
    • aimless vs ambitious
    • quitting vs persevering
  • Extraversion: how individuals interact with others and their social energy
    • reserved vs affectionate
    • loner vs joiner
    • quiet vs talkative
  • Agreeableness: individual's orientation toward social harmony and trust
    • antagonistic vs acquiescent
    • ruthless vs softhearted
    • suspicious vs trusting
  • Neuroticism: emotional stability and the tendency toward negative emotions
    • calm vs worrying
    • even-tempered, unemotional vs tempermental, emotional
    • hardy vs vulnerable

Evaluating the Trait Perspective

  • Strengths:
    • psychologists generally agree that people can be described and compared in terms of basic personality traits
    • the five-factor model has extensive research support
  • Limitations:
    • while trait theories may describe persoanlity well, they don't really explain it
    • no explanation of how or why individual differences develop
    • traits do not address important personality issues, such as basic motives that drive personality, the role of unconscious mental processes, how beliefs about the self would influence personality, or how psychological change and growth occur

Part 2: Factors Affecting Personality

Biological and Situational Influences on Personality

Many researchers believe that the big five traits are universal across cultures and are probably biologically based. Several international studies have found that personality traits fit, for the most part, within the overall structure of the Big Five

Many psychologists think that these traits correlate with specific patterns of brain activity

psychologist Colin DeYoung and his colleagues looked at whether personality traits were associated with specific brain structures

  • a consistent pattern of brain structural differences was found for extraersion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness
  • neuroticism was associated with a mixed pattern of structural differences in brain systems related to sensitivity to threat and punishment
  • no significant pattern of brain structure dfference ws notes for openness to experience

Behavioral genetics: interdisciplinary field that studies the effects of genes and herdity on behavior

  • twin studies and adoption studies have found that certain personality traits are substantially influenced by genetics, especially extraversion and neuroticism
    • as twins grow up and leave home, personalities can diverge somewhat, showing the influence of environment and experience, showing the influece of environment and experience. Influence of environmental factors on personality traits is at least equal to the influence of genetic factors
  • openness to experience, conscientiousness, and agreeableness are also influenced by genetics, although to a lesser degree than extraversion and neuroticism
  • the influence of environmental factors is a least equal to the influence of genetic factors

The "person-situation debate" considers whether personality or the situation (context) is a stronger predictor of behavior

  • human behavior is the result of a complex interaction between traits and situations
    • people modify their behaviors to conform to social norms and expectations
    • however, even when situational influences like social rules affect the expression of personality traits, the effect of personality remains

Research shows that personality traits appear to be relatively stable across situations

How Culture and Gender Influence Personality

Culture

Cross-cultural research demonstrates that people in a variety of cultures describe personality in terms of the same basic traits that are identified by the five-factor model. Even so, some researchers have noted some culturally specific patterns of behavior that don't easily fit into the five-factor model.

The HEXACO model may better represent personality traits cross-culturallly

HEXACO Model, by Michael Ashton and Lee Kibeom, is a six-factor model developed from a lexical approach, based on international and cross-cultural research

  • lexical appraoch: based on the idea that "the major dimensions of personality should be represented [with the] common . . . adjectives of natural languages." By studying a language, you can learn about a particular culture's understanding of personality
  • the HEXACO model added honesty-humility (sixth factor). Some characteristics of this factor fall under agreeableness in the Big Five
  • anger-related characteristics shifted from emotionality to the agreeableness factor (anger-related characteristics shifted from Big Five neuroticism factor (called emotionality in HEXACO) to HEXACO agreeableness factor)
  • the 6 factors of the HEXACO Model:
    1. Honesty-Humility
      • core characteristics: sincere, fair, and modest vs sly and boastful
      • relation to the Big Five: this is a unique factor; it is not included in the standard Big Five model
    2. Emotionality
      • core characteristics: emotional and fearful vs stable and brave
      • relation to the Big Five: similar to Neuroticism, but with a key distinction: high schores in HEXACO Emotionality do NOT include anger
    3. Extraversion
      • core characteristics: outgoing and talkative vs shy and quiet
      • relation to the Big Five: very similar to the Big Five trait of the same name
    4. Agreeableness
      • core characteristics: patient and gentle vs ill-tempered and stubborn
      • relation to Big Five: similar to Big Five Agreeableness, but with a specific shift: it inludes anger for those who receive low scores
    5. Conscientiousness
      • core characteristics: organized and careful vs sloppy and irresponsible
      • relation to the Big Five: very similar to the Big Five trait of the same name
    6. Openness to Experience
      • core characteristics: creative and unconventional versus shallow and unimaginative
      • relation to the Big Five: similar to the Big Five, but places a stronger emphasis on unconventionality
  • the HEXACO model aids in understanding the dark triad (tetrad) and light triad
    • Dark triad (tetrad): 3 (or 4) characteristics that have been linked with the concept of "evil"; characterize poeple who are more likley to cause harm/social problem
      • machiavellianism
      • low-level narcissism
      • low-level psychopathy
      • some added sadism
    • Light triad: centers on valuing the worth of every person and correlates with the honesty-humility factor of HEXACO

Gender

For most personality characteristics, there are no significant average differences between men and women. For example, women and men are, on average, very similar on such characteristics as impulsiveness and orderliness

However, mena dn women consistently differ on two personality dimension. The first is that women tend to be more nurturant than men. The second is that men tend to be more assertive than women (Palomares, 2009; Schmitt et al., 2008)

Some studies have examined gender differences in personality structure according to the the five-factor model

  • differences in neuroticism (specifically anxiety), extraversion (specifically assertiveness), and agreeableness (specifically tender-mindedness) were found between males and females. These differences correspond to gender stereotypes. Interestingly, the gender differences are more pronounced in cultures with more egalitarian gender roles and greater gender equity
    • Hyde, J.S. (2014). Gender Similarities and Differences
    • Schmitt, D.P., Long, A.E., McPhearson, A., O'Brien, K., Remmert, B., & Shah, S.H. (2017). Personality and gender differences in global perspective.

Stability and Change of Personality

stablility of the five factors:

  • Overall, research ahs show that traits are remarkably stable over time
  • seems to apply across the lifespan, except there appears to be an additional factor in childhood
    • the expansion to include "activity" as an independent fundamental factor is sometimes called the Little Six
    • Activity: reflects overall levels of energy, motion, and physical activity
      • the human personality structure drops this factor by late adolescence
  • throughout adulthood, slight declines in neuroticism are often observed. In addition,
  • there are often increases in agreeableness and conscientiousness over time with greater psychological maturity
    • Personality growth: increases in extraversion and conscientiousness, and decreases in neuroticism; linked to better career outcomes
  • however, in late adulthood, poeple tend to become less conscientiousness, less extraverted, and less emotionally stable (higher in neuroticism).
    • conscientiousness might decrease as people leave the workplace
    • extraversion might decrease as social circles become smaller
    • neuroticism might increase as health and financial insecurities rise

Part 3: Personality Assessment

Techniques of Personality Assessment

Psychological tests: assess a person's abilities, aptitudes, interests, or personality on the basis of a systematically obtained sample of behavior

Any psychological test is useful insofar as it achieves two basic goals:

  1. accurately and consistently reflects a person's characteristics on some dimension
  2. predicts a person's future psychological functioning or behavior

THere are two basic types of personality tests: projective tests and self-report inventories

Projective Tests

Projective test: type of personality test that involves a person's interpretation of an ambigious image

  • used to assess unconscious motives, conflicts, psychological defenses, and personality traits
  • developed out of psychoanalytical approaches
  • related to projection (defense mechanism)
  • examples:
    • Rorschach Inkblot Test
      presentation and interpretation of a series of black-and-white and colored inkblots; person described what they see in the inkblot
      • developed by Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach in 1921
    • Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)
      a projective personality test that involves creating stories about ambigious scenes; the person is thought to project his own motives, conflict, and other personality traits into the story he creates
      • developed by Henry Murray and colleagues

Evaluation of Projective Tests

  • Strengths:
    • provision of qualitative information about individual's psychological functioning
    • information can facilitate psychotherapy
  • Limitations:
    • highly subjective scoring
    • failure to produce consistent results
    • poor at predicting future behavior

Self-Report Inventories

Self-Report Inventories: psychological tests in which people's responses to standardized questions are compared with established norms

  • often called objective personality tests
  • example:
    • Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)
      a self-report inventory that assesses personality characteristics and psychological disorders; it is used to assess both normal and disturbed populations
      • most widely used self-report inventory
      • current version is MMPI -3
      • includes > 500 True / False items. Topics include:
        • social, political, sexual attitudes
        • physical and psychological health interpersonal relationships
        • abnormal thoughts and behaviors
      • includes "lying scales"
      • very good reliability and validity

Evaluation of Self-Report Inventories

  • Strengths:
    • standardization and use of established norms
    • reliability and validity are generally greater than those of projective tests
  • Limitations:
    • deliberate deception: people may be able to successfully create a false result
    • some people are prone to responding in a set way
    • people are not always accurate judges of their own behavior, attitudes, or attributes
