Part 1: The Basics of Social Cognition

Social Psychology: branch of psychology that studies how thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are influenced by the presence of other people and by the social and physical environment

Two key research areas of social psychology are:

  • Social Cognition: the mental processes people use to make sense of their social environments
    • These mental processes include:
      • Explicit Cognition: deliberate, conscious mental processes involved in perceptions, judgments, decisions, and reasoning
      • Implicit Cognition: automatic, non-conscious, mental processes that influence perceptions, judgments, decisions, and reasoning
    • research topics include person perception, attribution, attitudes
  • Social Influence: the effect of situation factors and other people on an individual's behavior
    • research topics include conformity, obedience, altruism, aggression

Relationships between Thought Processes and Social Behavior

Person Perception

the mental processes we use to form judgments and draw conclusions about characteristics and motives of others. It's an active, subjective process that always occurs in some interpersonal context

initially, you form very rapid first impressions based largely on looking at other people's faces

  • in a mere tenth of a second, you evaluate the other person's attractiveness, likeability, competence, trustworthiness, and aggressiveness

person perception is a very complex process involving numerous factors

  • we use mental shortcuts to simplify the process
  • mental frameworks help us organize observations, memories, and beliefs about people
  • for example, person perception considers norms for the social context

Social norms: the unwritten "rules", or expectations, for appropriate behavior in a particular sociall situation

Social categorization: mental process of categorizing people into groups (or social categories) on the basis of their shared characteristics

  • with limited time and info, we often rely on broad social categories

Implicit personality theory: a network of assumptions or beliefs about the relationships among various type of people, traits, or behaviors

  • Through previous social experience, we form cognitive schemas (mental frameworks) about the traits and behaviors associated with different "types" of people
    • one schema: Physical Attractiveness - "What is Beautiful is Good"
      • attractive people are perceived as more intelligent, happier, and better adjusted
      • attractive people also tend to be higher in self-esteem, intelligence, and other desireable personality traits
        • beginning in infancy and continuing throughout their lives, attractive people receive more attention and more favorable treatment from other people, such as parents, teachers, employers, and peers

the mental shortcuts (e.g., social categorization, implicit personality theories) tht simplify the process of person perception are natural, adaptive, and efficient cognitive processes for dealing with an extraordinarily complex social world

  • the ability to make rapid judgments about strangers is probably an evolved characteristic that conferred survival value
  • on the other hand, making rapid judgments based on superficial info can lead to embarassing and even costly errors

Attribution

the mental process of inferring the causes of people's behavior, including one's own. Also, the explanation given for a particular behavior

attributional biases affect our judgment about the causes of behaviors

common atttributional biases and explanatory patterns

  • Fundamental attribution error: tendency to spontaneously attribute the behavior of others to internal, personal characteristics, while ignoring or underestimating the role of external, situational factors
    • attributional bias that is common in individualistic cultures
    • palys role in blaming the victim
  • Blaming the victim: tendency to blame the innocent victim of misfortune for having somehow caused the problem or for not having taken steps to avoid or prevent it
    • this bias is reinforced by hindsight bias
  • Hindsight bias: the tendency to overestimate one's ability to have foreseen or predicted the outcome of an event
  • Just-world hypothesis: assumption that the world is fair and that, therefore, people get what they deserve and deserve what they get
  • Self-serving bias: people tend to credit themselvesfor thier successes (internal attributes) and to blame their failures on external circumstances (external attributes)
    • cross-cultural psychologists report that self-serving bias is not universal

Attitudes

a learned tendency to evaluate some object, person, or issue in a particular way

  • such evalutions may be psoitive, negative, or ambivalent
  • ex: What is your attitude towards the use of animals in research?
    • is it a good thing, a bad thing, or is your attitude mixed or conflicted

3 components of an attitude:

  • Cognitive: thoughts and conclusions about a given topic or situations
  • Emotional: feelings or emotions about a topic
  • Behavioral: your actions regarding the topic or situation

research indicates that factors that make people more likely to act in accordance to their attitudes:

  • you anticiapte a favorable outcome or response from others for doing so
  • your attitudes are extreme or are frequently expressed
  • you are very knowledgeable about the subjects
  • you have a vested interest in the subject

cognitive dissonance is an unpleasant state of psychological tension (dissonance) that occurs when two thoughts or perceptions (cognitions) are inconsistent

  • it typically results from the awareness that attitudes and behavior are in conflict
  • people seek ways to decrease the discomfort cause by the inconsistency
  • if you can rationalize or explain your behavior, the conflict (and the tension) is eliminated or avoided
  • if you can't explain your behavior, you may change your attitude so that it is in harmony with your behavior

Part 2: Social Cognition - Stereotypes, Prejudice, Discrimination

The Nature and Effects of Stereotyping, Prejudice, and Discrimination

Prejudice

negative attitudes toward people who belong to a specific social groups

  • racial and ethnic groups are far more alike than different
  • any differences that may exist between members of different racial and ethnic groups are far smaller than differences within a group
  • prejudice can occur with respect to many different kinds of social groups

Stereotype

a cluster of characteristics that are associated with all members of a specific social group

  • includes qualities that are unrelated to the objective criteria that define a given category
  • based on the assumption that people have certain characteristics because of their membership in a particular group
  • like other forms of social categorization, the tendency to stereotype is a natural cognitive process that simplifies social info, but it is problematic in several ways:
    • attributing a stereotypic cause for an outcome of an event cna blind us to the true cause
    • an insidious effect of stereotypes is stereotype threat, in which a person performs in accordance with his or her group's stereotype
    • once formed, stereotypes are hard to shake:
      • when confronted by evidence that contradicts a stereotype, pepole tend to discount that info
      • creating special cases (exceptions) allows people to maintain stereotypes in the face off contradictory evidence

two basic social categories often play a role in prejudice. people have a strong tendency to perceive others in terms of "Us" and "Them"

  • In-group: the social group to which one belongs (Us)
  • Out-group: the social group to which on does not belong (Them)

typically, we describe the members in out group as being quite varied, despite having enough features in common to belong to the same group

  • we tend to see member of the out-group as much more similar to one another
  • Out-group homogeneity effect: tendency to see members of the out-group as more similar to one another
  • In-group bias: the tendency to amke favorable, positive attributions for behaviors by members of our in-group and unfavorable, negative attributions for behaviors by members of out-groups

in combination, stereotypes and in-group-out-group bias form the cognitive basis for prejudicial attitudes

  • prejudice also have a strong emotional component, which is intensely negative and involves hatred, contempt, fear, and loathing
  • the behavioral component of prejudice can be displayed in the form of discrimination

Implicit Attitudes

preferences and biases toward particular groups that are automatic, spontaneous, unintentional, and often unconscious

  • sometimes, people who are not consciously prejudices against particular groups nevertheless respond in prejudiced ways
  • the Implicit Association Test, or IAT, was developed to measure implicit attitudes

Robber's Cave Study

conducted by Muzafer Sherif, Carolyn Wood Sherif, and colleagues, it helped clarify the conditions that produce intergroup conflict and harmony

  • boys who came to summer camp were randomly assigned to two groups. after a week of serparation, they met in a series of competitive games. a fierce rivarly quickly developed
  • peaceful, non-competitive activities did not reduce the hostilities
  • the researchers created a series of situations in which the groups would need to cooperate to achieve a common goal
    • after a series of joint efforts, the rivarly was diminished, and the groups became friends

some researchers questioned the applicability of these results to groups with intrinsic differences

Elliot Aronson and colleagues adapted the results of the Robbers Cave Study to a recently integrated elementary school

  • when mere contact between black and white children did not reduce tension and prejudice, Aronson reasoned that the competitive schoolroom atmosphere might be partly at fault
  • they developed the jigsaw classroom technique, which brought students together in small, ethnically diverse groups to work on a mutual project. results:
    • interdependence and cooperation replaced competition
    • the children demonstrated higehr self-esteem
    • negative stereotypes, prejudice, and intergroup hostility decreased

Part 3: Social Influence - Conformity and Obedience

obedience, conformity, and compliance in relation to behavior and their impact on the power of the situation

Conformity

adjusting opinions, judgments, or behaviors so that they match those of other people or the norms of a social group or situation

Solomon Asch (1951) Research

expirementally tested the question "Would people still conform to the group if the group opinion was clearly wrong?". How far would a person go to adjust his or her own perceptions and opinions so that they're in sync with the majority opinion?

Asch's research design

  • N > 100
  • one subject sits with five confederates
  • asked to rate which line matched a "standard" line
  • 18 trials for each subject
  • first two trials - confederates answer correctly
  • confederates give unanimously incorrect answers 12 of 18 trials

results: 76% of the subjects conformed to the group on at least one of the 12 critical trials; overall conformity rate was 37%

Normative Social Influence: refers to behavior that is motivated by the desire to gain social acceptance and approval

  • subjects desired to be liked and accepted by the group

Informational Social Influence: refers to behavior that is motivated by the desire to be correct

  • subjects reported having doubted their own perceptual abilities, which lead to their conformity
  • they did ot report seeing the lines the way the confederates had seen them

conformity is decreased when we have an ally in our dissent from the majority opinion, even if the dissenter's competence is questionable

Factors Increasing Likelihood of Conformity

  • Unanimous Group Size: you are facing a unanimous group of at least 4 or 5 people
  • Public Visibility: you must give your response in front of the group
  • No Prior Commitment: you have no already expressed a commitment to a different idea or opinion
  • Difficult Tasks: you find the task to be ambigious or difficult
  • Self-Doubt: you doubt your abilities or knowledge in the situation
  • Group Attraction: you are strongly attracted to a group and want to be a member of it

conformity is higher in collectivistic cultures than in individualistic cultures

  • Individualistic Cultures
    • tend to emphasize independence
    • conformity tends to carry a negative perception
  • Collectivistic Cultures
  • conforming while privately disagreeing tends to be regarded as socially appropriate tact or sensitivity
  • publicly challenging the judgments of others, particularly the judgment of members in of one's group, would be considered rude, tactless, and insensitive
  • conformity does not seem to carry the same negative connotation

Compliance and Obedience

whereas compliance is a response to a request, obedience is a response to a command

Obedience

the performance of a behavior in response to a direct command; typically the command is given by an authority figre or person of higher status

Stanley Milgrams Research:

Stanley Milgram's critical question: "Could a person be pressured by others into committing an immoral act, some actions that violated their own conscience, such as hurting a stranger?"

  • Milgram's research design:
    • N = 40 male participants
    • Confederate = 47 year old accountant trained for the part (the "learner")
    • with the help of the real subject, assigned to the role of "teacher", the experimenter straps the "learner" into the electric chair
    • the tacher and expirementer go to a room with a "shock generator"
    • the teacher is given a sample shock of 45 volts to establish the legitimacy of the generator
    • the teacher instructe to give progressively higher shocks for every mistake the learner makes
    • the higest level is labeled 450 volts with the descriptor, "X X X"
    • the learner protests more and more as the shock increases
    • expirementer continues to prompt obedience, 'The expirement requires that you continue'
  • Milgram asked psychiatrists, college students, and middle-class adults to make predictions about the outcome. None of those surveryed thought any participant would apply the full 450 volts
  • results:
    • two-thirds of Milgram's subjects - 26 of the 40 - were fully compliant and went to the full 450-volt level
    • of those ewho defied the experimenter, not one stopped before the 300-volt level

Milgram's study has been repeated many times in the United States and other countries with identical results

  • Milgram repeated the studuy many times with systematic variations, and he ultimately tested ~1,000 participants
  • in one replication, the participants were 40 women. this study adn other similar replications found no sex differences in obedience

Milgram and others identified several factors in the expiremental situation that promoted obedience:

  • a previously, well-established framework to obey
  • the situation, or context, in which obedience occured
  • the gradual, repetitive escalation of the task
  • the experimenter's behavior and reassurances
  • the physical ad psychological separation from the learner
  • suspicious that the learner was no actually receiving the shocks

Milgram's Expirement: Variations in Obedience

the percentage of prticiapnts who administered the maximum shock (450) volts across different expiremental variations:

  • Original study: 65%
  • Office building setting: when conducted in an officfe buiding instead of a univeristy setting, obedience dropped to 48%
  • Physical Proximity: when the teacher was required to force the learner's hand onto a "shock plate", obedience fell to 30%
  • Remote Authority: if the expirementer left the laboratory and gave orders over the phone, obedience was 23%
  • Perceived Authority Status: if the expirementer left and an "ordinary man" gave order to continue, obedience dropped significantly to 10%
  • Personal Choice: when the teacher was free to choose the shock level, only 3% administered the maximum shock

Ethical Concerns

some psychologists expressed ethical concerns because of the amount of stress experienced by the participants

Milgram included a "friendly reconciliation" in the design so that the "teacher" would be debriefed immediately to see that the "learner" was not harmed in any way

  • however, ~600 participants weren't told that the shocks were fake auntil about a year after their participation

Part 4: Social Influence - Prosocial/Antisocial and Group Dynamics

Influences Upon Prosocial and Antisocial Behaviors

Prosocial Behaviors

any behavior that helps another person, whether the underlying motive is self-servinf or selfless

  • soemtimes, we help people out of guilt, or in order to gain something, such as recognition, rewards, increased self-esteem, or having the favor returned
  • Altruism: helping another person with no expectation of personal reward or benefit

research shows that the factors increase helping:

  • personality factors
  • the "feel good, do good" effect
  • seeing other willing to help
  • perceiving the person as deserving help
  • knowing how to help
  • a personalized relationship
  • a dangerous situation

factors that decrease the likelihood of helping

  • the presence of other people: the bystander effect
    the greater the number of people present, the less likely each individual is to help someone in distress. reasons for the bystander effect:
    • Diffusion of responsibility: a phenomenon in which the presence of other people makes it less likely that any individual will help someone in distress; the obligation to intervene is shared among all the onlookers
    • Normative social influence: motivtion to behave in a socially acceptable way
  • vague or ambigious situations
  • when the personal costs for helping outweigh the benefits

Antisocial Behaviors

in psychology, antisocial does not mean shyness or avoiding people; it's behaviors disregard an dviolate the rights of others

Aggression: is any verbal or physical behavior intended to cause harm to other people

  • it must be perceived by the aggressor harmful to the other person adn the other person must not wish to be harmed

Biological theories of aggression:

  • research supports the belief that genetics play a significant role in the development of aggression
  • there are differences in the brain structures that regulate emotion, such as the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and limbic system
  • Biochemical Influences:
    • research shows that higher levels of the hormones testosterone, in both males and females, are associated with violent, aggressive behavior, and drug use
    • laboratory studies and naturalistic observation research show that rates of violence increase with the consumption of alcohol

Psychological factors:

  • Learning:
    • Bandura: violent people often mimic behavior they have seen; obervational learning
    • Bushman & others: exposure to media violence may increase aggressiv behavior
    • Hald & others: pronography viewinf linked to aggressive attitudes toward women
    • Willoughby & others: violent media has been linked to increased aggression
  • Frustration:
    • high temperature
    • stressful or annoying situations

Gender:

  • on average, males are more aggressive
  • consistent across cultures, men are more likely to display direct, especially physical, aggression. Females are just as aggressive as males in indirect aggression, such as gossiping and spreading rumors

Culture

  • cutures with significant income inequality also have higher rates of aggression
  • a culture of honor is one in which actions perceived as damaging your reputation must be addressed

How Group Dynamics Influence Behavior

Influence of Groups on Individual Behavior

Social Loafing and Social Striving

the tendency to expend less effort on a task when it is a group effort, especially when individua; efforts are difficult to observe

  • as a general rule, the greater number of people involved in a collective effort, the lower each individual's output
  • the responsibility for attaining the group goal is spread across all group members, resulting in reduced effort by each individual member
  • factors that reduce social loafing:
    • people in the group know each other
    • group is highly valued
    • task is meaningful or unqiue
    • women are generally less likely to engage in social loafing
  • cutural variations
    • social loafing is not only absent, but reversed in my collectivistic cultures
      • Social Striving: individuals work harder when in a group
        • in collectivistic cultures, group success tends tobe more highly valued then individual successes
        • moreoever, the social norms in collectivist cultures encourage a sense of social responsibility and hard work within groups

Social Facilitation and Deindividuation

Social Facilitation: the tendency for presence of other people to enhance individual performance. This is the effect for relatively simple or well-rehearsed tasks (if the task is complex or poorly learned, the presence of others can hinder performance)

Deindividuation: the reduction of self-awareness and inhibitions that can occur when a person is part of a group whose members feel anonymous

  • vandalism
  • online antisocial behavior: cheating in online games, trolling, aggression/cyberbullying
  • mob or riot behavior; social chaos

counteracting Deindividuation: heighten self-awareness
